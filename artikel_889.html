Adipose tissue has a central role in systemic energy balance and the pathogenesis of obesity and its sequelae1,2. In contrast to white adipose tissue, brown adipose tissue (BAT) uses energy to produce heat3. Detectable BAT in humans is associated with decreased prevalence of cardiometabolic diseases4. However, the complete spectrum of health conditions associated with BAT remains unknown, and high-throughput methods to quantify BAT activity in humans are lacking5,6. Here we leveraged big data and artificial intelligence to systematically define the clinical impact of BAT in a cohort of 101,243 patients from a large cancer centre. Our unbiased analyses revealed that, across all diagnoses, cardiovascular and psychiatric diseases were less prevalent in people with BAT. Using a machine learning approach, we semi-automatically quantified BAT activity in approximately 8,700 patients and demonstrated that higher BAT activity, especially in the axillary depot, is associated with lower odds of cardiometabolic diseases and healthier white fat distribution. People with BAT also exhibited more favourable glycaemic parameters, lipid profiles, inflammatory markers and echocardiographic indices. Analysis of medication usage demonstrated that angiotensin receptor blocker use was positively associated with BAT presence. As proof of concept, we showed that the angiotensin receptor blocker losartan increased brown adipocyte activity and whole-body energy expenditure. Altogether, our studies harnessing artificial intelligence and big data identify new clinical phenotypes linked to BAT and serve as a platform for drug repurposing to activate this tissue in humans.

Obesity is a major risk factor for type 2 diabetes (T2D), hypertension and cardiovascular disease2,7. Adipose tissue dysfunction is a hallmark of obesity and contributes to the pathophysiology of cardiometabolic diseases1. White adipose tissue (WAT) stores energy as triglyceride1 in subcutaneous and visceral depots, and visceral adiposity is a key feature of metabolically unhealthy obesity8. By contrast, brown adipose tissue (BAT) dissipates energy as heat (thermogenesis) and its activation in animal models protects against obesity and metabolic diseases3. However, the relevance of BAT to adult human physiology and whether it can be targeted therapeutically remain unclear5,6.

Since the landmark reports that adult humans have inducible BAT9,10,11,12, several clinical studies have aimed to recruit BAT through cold exposure or β-adrenergic agonists13,14,15,16,17. Although these interventions activate BAT and improve glucose homeostasis, their side effects and the small number of participants studied have limited their clinical impact5. More recently, our group described the largest reported cohort of people with BAT and demonstrated that the presence of BAT in 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) positron emission tomography (PET)–computed tomography (CT) (18F-FDG-PET–CT) scans obtained for clinical purposes and without previous stimulation was associated with reduced prevalence of cardiometabolic disease4. A caveat from this work is that patients were characterized based on BAT presence or absence, rather than by quantitative BAT metabolic activity. Like WAT, human BAT is localized in distinct anatomic depots18, but whether these depots uniquely affect health remains unknown. Moreover, most human BAT studies have focused on cardiometabolic outcomes, but how this tissue influences other organ systems and disease types remains largely unexplored. These limitations have hindered the identification of pharmacologic agents that promote BAT activity, and there are no approved, widely used medications to leverage the cardiometabolic benefits linked to this tissue.

In this study we expanded our cohort to more than 100,000 patients and performed an unbiased, comprehensive analysis across all diagnostic codes to identify new clinical phenotypes linked to BAT presence. Furthermore, we harnessed artificial intelligence (AI) to semi-automatically quantify total and depot-specific BAT metabolic activity from 18F-FDG-PET–CT scans. This allowed us to investigate associations between specific BAT depots, WAT distribution and cardiometabolic outcomes. Finally, we combined these approaches with medication use analyses to identify pharmacologic regulators of BAT and performed proof-of-concept experiments to validate losartan as an activator of brown adipocytes through a unique, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPARγ)-dependent mechanism.

To discover associations between BAT and diseases beyond the cardiometabolic system, we expanded our previously described cohort (Established BAT Cohort)4 by reviewing additional FDG-PET–CT scans taken from 2018 to 2024 at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center. Of these, 4.4% reported BAT presence and 4,778 patients (7.9%) had at least one scan positive for BAT (hereafter termed BAT positive (BAT+); Extended Data Fig. 1a). The remaining patients were designated BAT negative (BAT−). These newly identified participants were grouped in an Extension BAT Cohort (Extended Data Fig. 1a) that validated findings from our Established BAT Cohort4 demonstrating lower prevalence of cardiometabolic diseases in people who are BAT+ (Extended Data Fig. 1b). To increase statistical power, we merged the Established and Extension BAT Cohorts to create an Expanded BAT Cohort, consisting of 101,243 patients, of whom 8,897 were BAT+ and 92,346 were BAT−. Each patient was assigned an index scan as a reference point for clinical data collection (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Similar to previous findings4, BAT was more prevalent in women in the Expanded BAT Cohort, and its prevalence decreased with age, higher ambient temperature at the time of the scan and increasing body mass index (BMI) (Extended Data Fig. 1c–f).

Given the influence of these variables on BAT prevalence, we used propensity score matching (PSM) to create a cohort of patients in the BAT+ (n = 8,897) and BAT− (n = 17,405) groups balanced across age, sex, BMI and ambient temperature (Extended Data Fig. 1g,h). As patients in our cohort come from a cancer centre, we extracted extensive cancer-associated data (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2) for each patient in our PSM cohort, including site, stage and treatment. We confirmed that certain cancer sites were more frequent (for example, breast and oral cavity), less frequent (for example, digestive) or had no association (for example, skin, urinary) with BAT presence (Table 1). As in our previous report, some cancer treatments were used more frequently in the BAT+ group4 (Table 1). Consequently, all cancer-associated variables were controlled for unless otherwise stated for the remainder of this study. We then performed multi-variable logistic regression in this new, expanded PSM cohort and confirmed the association between BAT presence and decreased prevalence of select cardiometabolic diseases4 (Extended Data Fig. 1i). Given the sexual dimorphism in BAT presence and cardiometabolic traits, we performed separate analyses in women and men and found that BAT was associated with decreased odds of cardiometabolic diseases in both sexes in our PSM and Extension BAT Cohorts (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b).

Next, we used an unbiased approach to establish associations between BAT and diseases across all organ systems in our PSM cohort (Fig. 1a). After extraction of International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code data and initial screening analysis (Fig. 1a), 104 of 511 codes exhibited significant, non-adjusted associations with BAT (Supplementary Table 3). Over-representation analysis revealed that cardiovascular and psychiatric diagnoses were enriched significantly in their association with BAT (Fig. 1b). We then ran a multi-variable logistic regression for each of the 104 codes to establish associations with BAT, adjusting for multiple co-variates. This resulted in 98 codes that were significant after adjustment, with 92 showing lower incidence in patients in the BAT+ group (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 4).

Among the top 20 codes with lower prevalence in the BAT+ group, the two most significant were T2D (odds ratio (OR) 0.42; 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.35–0.50; P < 0.001) and chronic ischaemic heart disease (OR 0.53; 95% CI 0.44–0.62; P < 0.001); other cardiometabolic codes such as disorders of lipoprotein metabolism (OR 0.76; 95% CI 0.70–0.83; P < 0.001) and primary hypertension (OR 0.79; 95% CI 0.73–0.85; P < 0.001) were also present. We also identified associations with other disease systems, including decreased odds of respiratory failure (OR 0.46; 95% CI 0.37–0.57; P < 0.001), ascites (OR 0.51; 95% CI 0.43–0.62; P < 0.001), anxiety disorders (OR 0.78; 95% CI 0.73–0.84; P < 0.001), depressive episode (OR 0.68; 95% CI 0.61–0.76; P < 0.001) and sleep disorders (OR 0.77; 95% CI 0.71–0.85; P < 0.001) in patients who were BAT+ (Fig. 1c). Among codes with higher prevalence in the BAT+ group, five of six were skin diagnoses (Extended Data Fig. 2c). BAT+ status also trended toward being associated with a higher prevalence of osteoporosis, although this did not reach statistical significance in the adjusted analysis (Extended Data Fig. 2c).

We used a newly developed machine learning-based approach (TriDFusion (3DF))19 to semi-automatically measure total and depot-specific BAT metabolic activity (defined as product of the mean standard 18F-FDG uptake value (standardized uptake value (SUV)) and BAT volume in millilitres) from most patients with BAT+ status (n = 8,709; 97.9% of the BAT+ group (Methods)) in our Expanded BAT Cohort (Fig. 2a,b). Among the six previously described BAT depots, the supraclavicular had the highest prevalence and activity (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). Total BAT metabolic activity was correlated inversely with age and was higher in women (Extended Data Fig. 3c–f). As cardiometabolic diagnoses exhibited the strongest link with BAT (Fig. 1b), we stratified our 8,709 patients with BAT activity measurements into tertiles and examined whether increasing total and depot-specific BAT activity was associated with decreased odds of cardiometabolic diseases using patients in the BAT− group as a reference. Indeed, higher total BAT metabolic activity was associated with a stepwise reduction in the prevalence of T2D (P < 0.001), atrial fibrillation and/or atrial flutter (P < 0.001), coronary artery disease (CAD; P < 0.001) and congestive heart failure (P < 0.001), even after controlling for age, BMI, sex and outside temperature (Fig. 2c).

BAT in humans is localized in several depots, which could have distinct functions depending on their location or could contain different cell types20,21,22. Until now, it was not feasible to perform large-scale analyses with depot-level resolution, so we used our AI tool to compare the association between cardiometabolic diagnoses and BAT metabolic activity in each of the six different BAT depots compared with patients in the BAT− group (Fig. 2c). Within the most prevalent depot, the supraclavicular, increasing BAT activity was associated with clear, dose-dependent reduced odds of T2D (P < 0.001) and CAD (P < 0.001) in all tertiles. In both the cervical and supraclavicular depots, higher BAT activity was associated with lower odds of hypertension, but this effect was lost for patients with highest BAT activity (tertile 3) (Fig. 2c). Of note, increasing axillary BAT activity was associated with significant reductions in the prevalence of all cardiometabolic diseases queried (including cerebrovascular disease), with a clear stepwise decrease in the risk of hypertension, in contrast to most other depots (Fig. 2c).

We carried out similarly controlled analyses within patients with BAT+ status, using patients in tertile 1 as a reference. Given the low prevalence of cardiometabolic disease in people with BAT, the associations were not as statistically powerful, but we identified similar trends (Extended Data Fig. 3g). Increasing total BAT metabolic activity was associated with lower odds of T2D (P = 0.009) and CAD (P = 0.03) in this analysis. Within the cervical depot, higher BAT activity was associated with a slight reduction in the incidence of CAD (P = 0.04), but increased odds of hypertension (P < 0.001). Increasing mediastinal BAT activity was associated exclusively with reduced CAD prevalence (P = 0.004)—an interesting finding given its anatomical location. Of note, higher axillary BAT activity also showed the greatest effects in this analysis, associating with reductions in T2D (P = 0.03), congestive heart failure (P = 0.02) and hypertension (P = 0.005) (Extended Data Fig. 3g).

Given the well described association between WAT distribution and cardiometabolic disease8, we used the CT portion of each 18F-FDG-PET–CT scan to semi-automatically measure the subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) and visceral adipose tissue (VAT) area. We were able to obtain these measurements and the SAT to VAT ratio in 8,040 patients in the BAT+ group (92.3% of patients with BAT from our PSM cohort; Extended Data Fig. 4a). We then performed linear regression analyses between BAT activity and WAT distribution parameters. From here on, most variables were log transformed due to non-normal data distribution and, consequently, coefficients (β) derived from the linear regression appear numerically small, as they reflect proportional relationships rather than raw changes. Total BAT metabolic activity showed a positive association with SAT area (P < 0.001), negative association with VAT area (P < 0.001) and positive association with SAT to VAT ratio (Fig. 2d,e). Given our previous findings, we assessed the association between depot-specific BAT activity and WAT distribution. Supraclavicular (Extended Data Fig. 4b,c), mediastinal (Extended Data Fig. 4f,g) and abdominal (Extended Data Fig. 4j,k) BAT metabolic activity behaved similarly to total BAT, exhibiting positive associations with SAT area and SAT to VAT ratio, and negative associations with VAT area. Paraspinal (Extended Data Fig. 4h,i) and cervical (Extended Data Fig. 4d,e) BAT (the latter exhibiting an association with increased odds of hypertension) were associated positively with SAT and VAT. Finally, axillary BAT (Fig. 2f,g), which was associated most strongly with decreased odds of cardiometabolic diseases, exhibited the most significant negative association with VAT area and, consequently, the strongest positive association with SAT to VAT ratio. Collectively, these data highlight the unique phenotypes linked to different BAT depots.

To further investigate clinical correlates of BAT we extracted an expanded battery of available laboratory tests and imaging studies from patients in our PSM cohort (Extended Data Fig. 5a) and performed analyses to identify differences between patients in the BAT+ and BAT− groups (Supplementary Table 5). Patients with BAT had lower glucose (P < 0.001), haemoglobin A1C (HbA1c) (P < 0.001), triglycerides (P < 0.001) and triglyceride-glucose index, as well as higher high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol (HDL-C) levels (P < 0.001), even after adjusting for confounders (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 5b). Paradoxically, patients with BAT also had higher total cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). However, remnant cholesterol (total cholesterol − LDL − HDL)—an independent risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease23—was significantly lower in patients with BAT (Extended Data Fig. 5b). Finally, patients with BAT exhibited lower circulating brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) (Extended Data Fig. 5b).

To expand on these observations, we used semi-quantitative BAT data from our AI tool to establish associations between continuous clinical parameters and BAT activity. We found an inverse association between total BAT metabolic activity and glucose levels (P < 0.001), HbA1c (P = 0.006), systolic blood pressure (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3b), mean arterial blood pressure (P < 0.001) and BNP (P = 0.01) and a trend with diastolic blood pressure (P = 0.12) (Extended Data Fig. 5c). We did not observe statistically significant associations between total BAT activity and lipids (Extended Data Fig. 5c).

Given the absence of large-scale studies linking BAT status with cardiac imaging in humans, and the association of BAT with higher carotid elasticity in a small prospective study24, we extracted available echocardiogram data from patients in our PSM cohort and performed association analyses. In alignment with previous findings25, patients with BAT had significantly lower left ventricular mass index (P < 0.001), left ventricular posterior wall thickness in diastole (LVPWD) (P < 0.001), relative wall thickness (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3c), pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) and mitral valve E/e’ ratio, with no changes in ejection fraction, left atrial volume index and mitral valve E/A ratio (Extended Data Fig. 5d). Similarly, increasing BAT metabolic activity was associated inversely with PASP (P < 0.001), LVPWD (P = 0.01), relative wall thickness (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3d), mitral valve E/e’ ratio (P = 0.002) and positively with mitral valve E/A ratio (P < 0.001) (Extended Data Fig. 5e). Other echocardiographic parameters did not exhibit associations with semi-quantitative BAT activity (Extended Data Fig. 5e). These data demonstrate that BAT presence and activity associate with decreased markers of left ventricular remodelling.

With regard to other medical conditions, patients with BAT had lower transaminase levels (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3e), lower alkaline phosphatase and higher albumin, and a lower percentage of patients had increased FIB-4 scores—a surrogate for the risk of advanced liver fibrosis26 (Extended Data Fig. 5f). Parameters of systemic inflammation were also lower in patients with BAT, including total neutrophils (P < 0.001), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3e), platelets and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (Extended Data Fig. 5g). There was also a trend towards lower C-reactive protein in patients with BAT, with no changes in total white blood cells or lymphocytes (Extended Data Fig. 5g). We did not observe changes in renal or thyroid function in patients in the BAT+ versus BAT− groups (Extended Data Fig. 5h). As patients with BAT showed a trend towards higher osteoporosis prevalence, available bone density scans were analysed. Patients with BAT had lower vertebral T-scores compared with patients in the BAT− group (P = 0.0026), with no changes in femoral neck T-scores (Fig. 3f).

To unravel pharmacologic determinants of BAT presence, we extracted home medication data 1 month before the index scan in patients from our PSM cohort (Extended Data Fig. 6a). In total, 125 individual medications were assessed for associations with BAT status after exclusions (Extended Data Fig. 6a). Multi-variable logistic regression analyses identified that 39 of 125 medications exhibited a significant association with BAT status (Supplementary Table 6). Given that antibiotics are generally used in short courses, we excluded them from our list of chronic medications (Extended Data Fig. 6b). However, as modulation of the microbiome by antimicrobials could affect thermogenic fat27, we performed a focused analysis on antibiotics and found that trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and several cephalosporins had positive associations with BAT status (Extended Data Fig. 6b).

Among our 39 candidate medications, we identified agents with known inhibitory effects on human BAT, such as β-blockers9,28. Specifically, atenolol (OR 0.42; 95% CI 0.27–0.64; P < 0.001) and propranolol (OR 0.38; 95% CI 0.20–0.74; P = 0.02) (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 6c) exhibited negative associations with BAT presence. Other known negative regulators of BAT, namely, selective serotonin re-uptake inhibitors (SSRIs)29 such as fluoxetine (OR 0.33; 95% CI 0.21–0.51; P < 0.001), escitalopram (OR 0.60; 95% CI 0.48–0.75; P < 0.001) and sertraline (OR 0.57; 95% CI 0.44–0.75; P < 0.001) (Fig. 4a), were also associated negatively with BAT. Given that people with BAT had a lower prevalence of psychiatric disease, we performed analyses on psychiatric medications controlling for relevant psychiatric diagnoses (Supplementary Table 7), and demonstrated that SSRIs retain their negative association with BAT (Extended Data Fig. 6d). Among medications shown previously to activate BAT, we found that dexamethasone30 was associated positively with BAT presence (OR 1.22; 95% CI 1.10–1.36; P = 0.001; Fig. 4a). Mirabegron—a β3-adrenergic agonist that promotes BAT activity in humans13,31,32—also showed a trend towards being associated with BAT positivity (Extended Data Fig. 6e). β2-adrenergic agonists (for example, inhaled salbutamol), which could promote BAT activity in humans33,34, did not show a significant association (Supplementary Table 6). These findings demonstrate that our platform can identify most known modulators of human BAT and could uncover new pharmacological regulators of this tissue.

Among medications associated positively with BAT, we found hormonal modifiers (letrozole, leuprolide), antihypertensives (hydrochlorothiazide, amlodipine, losartan) and oral cancer drugs (osimertinib, lenalidomide). Based on expression of the target receptor and/or pathway in adipocytes35, we focused on angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) as potential BAT-promoting drugs. The ARBs losartan (OR 1.36; 95% CI 1.13–1.63; P = 0.006) and valsartan (OR 1.52; 95% CI 1.14–2.02; P = 0.02) had significant positive associations with BAT+ status, whereas irbesartan exhibited a non-statistically significant trend in the same direction (Fig. 4b).

As proof of concept, we tested whether losartan increased the activity of differentiated brown adipocytes in vitro. First, we assessed whether losartan regulated uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1)—the main mediator of BAT thermogenesis. Losartan at low doses (10 μM) had no effect, whereas higher doses (50 μM and 100 μM) significantly increased Ucp1 RNA levels in immortalized mature brown adipocytes (Extended Data Fig. 7a). Treatment with losartan at 50 μM induced an increase in Ucp1 comparable with noradrenaline (NE)—a β-adrenergic agonist and known thermogenic activator (Fig. 4c); moreover, co-treatment with losartan and NE produced a synergistic effect on Ucp1 (Fig. 4c). Losartan also increased RNA levels of other thermogenic genes such as Pgc1a (also known as Ppargc1a), Elovl3 and Cidea (Extended Data Fig. 7b). A similar effect was seen in subcutaneous adipocytes enriched for beige adipocyte progenitors and, to a lesser extent, subcutaneous white adipocytes (Extended Data Fig. 7c). Losartan increased UCP1 protein in primary mature brown adipocytes at baseline and upon NE stimulation (Fig. 4d and Extended Data Fig. 7d). Finally, we assessed oxygen consumption rate in primary mature brown adipocytes and demonstrated that 72 h pretreatment with losartan significantly increased NE-dependent uncoupled respiration—a proxy for thermogenesis (Fig. 4e).

Similar to reports in human adipocytes36, angiotensin II decreased Ucp1 expression in mature brown adipocytes, whereas co-treatment with losartan rescued and further increased Ucp1 expression, suggesting a possible angiotensin receptor 1 (AT1R)-independent mechanism (Extended Data Fig. 7e). We treated mature brown adipocytes with EXP3174, the active losartan metabolite (10–40% higher affinity for ATR1 than the parent compound) and EXP3179—a losartan metabolite with no affinity for ATR1 (refs. 37,38). Unexpectedly, EXP3179, but not EXP3174, increased Ucp1 expression at baseline and in response to NE (Extended Data Fig. 7f,g). To further confirm that losartan acts independently of AT1R signalling, we knocked down At1r (Extended Data Fig. 7h) and showed that losartan still increased Ucp1 expression. Knockdown of the receptor alone also seemed to affect Ucp1 expression (Extended Data Fig. 7i), consistent with both AT1R-dependent and independent mechanisms.

Previous studies have suggested that some ARBs could act as direct PPARγ agonists39. We found that losartan and the inactive metabolite EXP3179, but not the active metabolite EXP3174, increased RNA levels of the classic PPARγ target aP2 with and without NE stimulation (Extended Data Fig. 7j–l). Losartan also decreased RNA levels of PPARγ with no changes to adiponectin (Extended Data Fig. 7m) Thus, using a luciferase reporter system in HEK293T cells, we tested whether losartan and its inactive metabolite EXP3179 activate PPARγ directly. Increasing concentrations of losartan or EXP3179, but not EXP3174, resulted in augmented reporter activity (Fig. 4f). To test directly whether PPARγ mediates the increased Ucp1 observed with losartan and EXP3179 in brown adipocytes, we co-treated cells with these compounds and a pharmacological inhibitor of PPARγ, GW9662, which binds the ligand-binding domain and prevents agonist-induced activation. Indeed, blocking PPARγ significantly attenuated the effects of losartan and its inactive metabolite on Ucp1 RNA induction at baseline and upon NE stimulation (Fig. 4g and Extended Data Fig. 7n,o). Altogether, these data demonstrate that losartan increases UCP1 expression in brown adipocytes independently of AT1R signalling and by stimulating PPARγ transcriptional activity.

Finally, we investigated the effects of losartan on BAT physiology in vivo. Treating wild-type mice for 2 weeks with losartan at room temperature resulted in increased expression of Ucp1 and other thermogenic genes in BAT with no significant changes to adipogenesis genes (Extended Data Fig. 8a,b). This was also observed at the protein level (Extended Data Fig. 8c,d). To functionally assess BAT thermogenic capacity, we acclimated losartan-treated mice and controls to thermoneutrality and then treated them acutely with the β3-agonist CL-316,243 (Extended Data Fig. 8e). BAT thermogenesis in response to β3 agonism (assessed by thermal camera) was higher in mice also receiving losartan compared with controls (Fig. 4h,i and Extended Data Fig. 8f).

To further assess the effects of losartan on whole-body energy expenditure, indirect calorimetry was performed in wild-type mice pre-treated with losartan in their drinking water for 10 days at room temperature (Extended Data Fig. 9a). Losartan-treated mice exhibited increased energy expenditure, O2 consumption and CO2 production, with no changes to their respiratory exchange ratio (RER), physical activity, food intake, body weight, fat mass or lean mass (Extended Data Fig. 9a–l). To examine the effects of losartan on cold stress, we pre-treated a separate cohort of wild-type mice at room temperature as above and performed indirect calorimetry at 4 °C (Extended Data Fig. 10a). Upon cold-induced BAT activation, losartan-treated mice again exhibited increased energy expenditure, O2 consumption and CO2 production, with no changes in RER (Fig. 4j,k and Extended Data Fig. 10b–e). This was independent of body weight, as assayed by analysis of covariance (Fig. 4l). There were no differences in locomotor activity and a trend towards increased food intake in losartan-treated mice (Extended Data Fig. 10f–h), which probably explains why body weight did not change (Extended Data Fig. 10i). Moreover, exposing mice treated with losartan to cold resulted in significantly increased BAT Ucp1 (Fig. 4m), as well as smaller lipid droplets in brown adipocytes (Fig. 4n). We observed similar histologic findings in inguinal fat; however, there was no statistically significant increase in Ucp1 expression (Extended Data Fig. 10j,k). Taken together, these data indicate that losartan treatment results in enhanced BAT activation and increased whole-body energy expenditure.

Despite substantial advances, the therapeutic potential for targeting BAT to manage metabolic disease remains preliminary5,6. This retrospective study explored associations between BAT and human health in the largest cohort reported so far. We validated the strong link between BAT and cardiovascular diseases, while uncovering a previously undescribed association with psychiatric diseases. Our new machine learning approach allowed us to quantify depot-specific BAT metabolic activity rapidly and establish that the degree of BAT activity is linked to cardiometabolic health and healthier fat distribution. Finally, medication usage analysis uncovered a positive association between ARB use and BAT presence. Proof-of-concept in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that losartan activates brown adipocytes and promotes whole-body energy expenditure, demonstrating that our systems approach can identify pharmacologic agents that could be repurposed to activate BAT.

Numerous studies in mice have shown that BAT activation protects against obesity and hyperglycaemia through increased energy expenditure, clearance of circulating substrates and secretion of beneficial hormones and metabolites40,41,42. In humans, cold exposure and β-adrenergic agonists increase BAT metabolic activity, which correlates with improved glucose homeostasis13,14. Our study confirms that, among all diagnoses, BAT is associated primarily with decreased prevalence of cardiovascular diseases, improved glycaemic, lipid and inflammatory profiles, and evidence of decreased pathological cardiac remodelling. Our large-scale, depot-specific analysis allowed us to identify axillary BAT as being linked most strongly with beneficial metabolic outcomes and healthier WAT distribution. These findings highlight the heterogeneity of thermogenic adipose tissues in humans and the importance of dissecting their cellular and molecular characteristics20,21,22.

AI tools such as ours will allow other groups to analyse BAT semi-quantitatively in their cohorts and expand these findings to other populations worldwide. This approach continues to rely on BAT glucose uptake on 18F-FDG-PET–CT scans, which may or may not correlate with BAT temperature and thermogenic capacity43,44, and could be influenced significantly by insulin resistance45. To this point, we found that insulin-sensitizing medications such as metformin46 were associated negatively with BAT presence (adjusting for diabetes status), and medications like olanzapine47, which promote insulin resistance, were linked positively to BAT. This indicates that the clinical findings in our study cannot be explained simply by altered insulin sensitivity.

Beyond metabolic diseases, BAT presence was associated with a lower prevalence of anxiety and depression. This could be explained by studies linking healthier metabolic profiles and decreased visceral adiposity to lower rates of psychiatric disease48,49,50. Small studies have linked cold exposure—a known activator of BAT—to improved mental health51,52. Furthermore, BAT prevalence decreased with SSRI use, confirming findings from a recent, prospective human study29. Future studies focused on how BAT affects mood and behaviour, and the role of anti-depressants in this crosstalk are warranted. BAT presence was associated with lower prevalence of liver disease and improved hepatic function, consistent with previous studies53,54. Conversely, patients in the BAT+ group showed a trend towards higher prevalence of osteoporosis and decreased lumbar bone mineral density, contrasting with previous reports linking cold-activated BAT with higher bone mineral density55. Although mouse studies suggest an endocrine connection between brown fat and bone56, we cannot rule out that the observed association is specific to our cancer-centre-based cohort. Future trials on human BAT therapeutics should include assessment of bone health.

Our study uses a reverse translational approach, starting with a large human dataset that informs proof-of-concept experiments, to bridge the gap between predominantly mouse-based discoveries and human brown fat biology. Through this platform, we identified several ARBs that are associated positively with BAT and demonstrate that the medication used most widely in this family, losartan, directly activates brown adipocytes through PPARγ, independently of AT1R signalling. In vivo treatment with losartan at baseline and during a cold challenge resulted in increased whole-body energy expenditure. By contrast, a previous report described a role for losartan in beiging of white fat through AT1R-dependent apelin secretion57. Compared with losartan, its inactive metabolite EXP3179 exhibits higher PPARγ agonism in vitro, and it probably mediates most of the effects of losartan on BAT in vivo. In humans, chronic treatment with losartan resulted in increased serum levels of EXP3179 with a peak 2 h after treatment, and increased expression of PPARγ gene targets in circulating monocytes37. Treatment with ARBs has been associated with reductions in newly diagnosed diabetes58, which could be linked to its effects on brown fat.

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design highlights the need for large prospective studies; however, we controlled for several clinical confounders and validated the association of losartan with BAT experimentally. Second, 18F-FDG-PET–CT scans were obtained without previous cold exposure, potentially underestimating BAT prevalence. Third, our convenience cohort was derived from a cancer centre and, despite controlling extensively for cancer-related variables, we cannot exclude effects from tumour types or cancer therapies. For instance, BAT is linked to higher breast cancer prevalence, lower odds of gastrointestinal malignancies and higher usage of cancer treatments such as mitotic inhibitors. Studies in mice have suggested activation of BAT reduces cancer progression by decreasing available circulating glucose for tumour metabolism59. To our knowledge, there is no literature demonstrating direct effects of mitotic inhibitors or other cancer therapies on brown adipocytes, and future studies of these associations are warranted. Until a reliable biomarker or non-invasive test for BAT is established, a large, prospective study involving 18F-FDG-PET–CT in the non-oncologic setting is highly implausible. At present, a large PSM cohort is the most feasible approach to assess BAT and clinical phenotypes comprehensively in humans.

In conclusion, we describe new associations between BAT and clinical outcomes through big data and AI-based analyses. Our systems-approach provides a resource for the field and can be extended to study other regulators of BAT such as genetics. Finally, our proof-of-concept studies demonstrate that losartan activates brown adipocytes through a PPARγ dependent mechanism, raising the possibility that this agent could be repurposed to activate BAT in humans.

This retrospective case–control study followed institutional guidelines and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSK), protocol number 17-581. The requirement for informed consent was waived because the study involved secondary analysis of previously acquired PET–CT imaging and clinical data, posed no more than minimal risk to participants and could not be conducted practically with individual informed consent. All patient data were deidentified before use.

We previously established a cohort of patients with BAT by analysing 18F-FDG-PET–CT scans performed at MSK from June 2009 through March 20184. Patients who were positive for BAT in at least one scan were termed BAT+, whereas the remaining patients were grouped as BAT−. To expand this cohort, we established a new Extension BAT Cohort by following a similar strategy. We reviewed all 18F-FDG-PET–CT scans performed at MSK from patients aged 18 years and above from April 2018 through July 2024. People who were positive for BAT on at least one scan were termed BAT+, whereas the remaining patients were grouped as BAT− (Extended Data Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 2). For each patient in both groups, an index scan was designated: in patients with BAT reported on at least one 18F-FDG-PET–CT scan, the first scan that reported BAT was defined as the index scan. In patients without BAT on any scan, the first 18F-FDG-PET–CT scan served as the index scan. We collected demographic, BMI, outside temperature data and pertinent ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes corresponding to cardiometabolic diseases4 (1 year after the index scan) (Supplementary Table 8) from the institutional EHR. Average outdoor temperatures in New York City were obtained from the US National Weather Service. An initial logistic regression analysis was performed on patients in the BAT+ versus BAT− groups who belonged exclusively to the Extension BAT Cohort to establish associations between BAT presence and cardiometabolic diseases. As the association of BAT presence with reduced prevalence of cardiometabolic disease was confirmed in the Extension BAT Cohort (Extended Data Fig. 1b), we proceeded to merge the Established BAT Cohort with the Extension BAT Cohort to create an Expanded BAT Cohort (Extended Data Fig. 1a). If a patient was present in both cohorts, data from the Established BAT Cohort were used.

To create a balanced study cohort derived from the Expanded BAT Cohort, patients in the BAT+ and BAT− groups were matched using PSM. Propensity scores were estimated using a non-parsimonious multi-variable logistic regression model with BAT status (presence or absence) as the dependent variable and age, sex, BMI and temperature at time of scan as co-variates. Matching was performed using a greedy nearest neighbour matching algorithm without replacement, with a calliper width set to 0.2 and a matching ratio of 1:2. This resulted in a final study cohort of 26,302 (8,897 BAT+ and 17,405 BAT−). Balance diagnostics were performed to assess the success of the matching procedure using standardized mean differences for all variables included in the propensity score model before and after matching. Postmatching standardized mean differences within the range of −0.10 to 0.10 indicated adequate balance between the BAT+ and BAT− groups across all measured co-variates, supporting the validity of the PSM design for causal inference.

We collected pertinent clinical data for each patient as described previously4. In brief, for cancer site, we included the specific cancer diagnostic code (ICD-0) listed closest to the index scan date (Supplementary Table 1). Data referring to cancer treatment were collected if administered within 90 days before the index scan and classified as previously4 (Supplementary Table 2). Pertinent ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes corresponding to cardiometabolic diseases4 (Supplementary Table 8) and all other codes were extracted up to 1 year after the index scan in patients from our PSM cohort. Codes were selected to represent non-paediatric, non-obstetric, adult disorders as explained below.

For laboratory values, clinical indices and imaging studies, we extracted available data up to 1 year after the index scan in our PSM cohort. Analyses were performed in BAT+ and BAT− patients with available values. To identify individual pharmacological agents associated with BAT presence, we extracted medications in the home medication list and/or medications with an active pharmacy prescription in the EHR from each patient belonging to our PSM cohort. We included only medications listed up to 1 month before the index scan for each patient. We excluded pharmacological agents with unlikely systemic effects, such as those with topical, ophthalmic, nasal and otic administration. Antimicrobials were excluded as they are generally prescribed in short courses. Medications classified as ‘as needed’ and herbal supplements were excluded. Medications with low frequency counts (present in fewer than 40 patients) were excluded. A final list of medications included in the analyses can be found in Supplementary Table 9. These medications were also grouped by general categories as described in Supplementary Table 9.

To explore unbiased associations between BAT and other diseases, we extracted from the EHR diagnostic codes (ICD-9 and ICD-10) listed up to 1 year after the index scan from patients belonging to our PSM cohort. To select the codes for extraction, we grouped ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes based on their first three letters and/or digits. ICD-10 codes belonging to general groupings: A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H, I, J, K, L, M, N and R were included as they group the medical pathologies most likely to be found in a non-paediatric, non-obstetric, adult cancer population. A similar rationale was used for the selection of ICD-9 codes. A total of 1,157 ICD-10 codes and 626 ICD-9 codes were queried for extraction from the EHR.

After code extraction, to avoid redundancy we merged ICD-9 codes with their corresponding ICD-10 codes based on a manual review of each code. The nomenclature of the ICD-10 code was maintained for downstream analysis in cases where an ICD-10 and ICD-9 code were merged. ICD-9 codes that did not have a corresponding ICD-10 code were maintained with their nomenclature. This left us with a total of 1,209 codes between individual ICD-9 and merged ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes.

To further filter diagnostic codes for analyses, we excluded codes with low frequencies (present in fewer than 40 patients) and those referring to a cancer diagnosis (Supplementary Table 1) given all patients had a cancer site assigned already. This left us with a final list of 511 diagnostic codes. Given the large number of codes, we initially performed a screening analysis using a univariable logistic regression analysis to identify unadjusted associations of the 511 with the presence of BAT. A total of 104 codes were associated significantly with BAT status in this analysis. Subsequently, we analysed each of these 104 codes using a multi-variable regression analysis to assess their individual association with BAT, while controlling for age, sex, BMI, ambient temperature, self-reported race and ethnicity, smoking history, family history of cardiovascular disease, cancer site, cancer stage, cancer treatment and general groups of outpatient medications (Extended Data Fig. 1a).

The 3DF workflow19 used PET and CT images in DICOM format. Using information from the DICOM header regarding image orientation and patient spatial positioning, the PET image was resampled to match the dimensions of the CT image through linear interpolation. Both PET and CT images were then converted into the .nrrd file format and processed by nnU-net. The nnU-net model produced a mask prediction where each pixel value corresponds to an anatomical site: 1 for cervical, 2 for supraclavicular, 3 for mediastinal, 4 for paraspinal, 5 for axillary and 6 for abdominal regions. The mask predictions were interpreted by 3DF, and contours were generated. These contours were down-sampled to match the original PET image dimensions, and metrics from the original PET for each anatomical site were exported to an Excel master file. The 3DF process was executed in batch mode, with several instances running simultaneously to efficiently segment a large cohort. We used this method to quantify BAT metabolic activity in 8,709 patients in the BAT+ group from our cohort. A small portion of patients (n = 188) in the BAT+ group could not be quantified due to differences in field of view between the CT and PET image.

The 3DF19,60 abdominal adipose tissue workflow was performed on axial CT images at the L3–L4 intervertebral disc level. The target slice was identified automatically using TotalSegmentator61 vertebral segmentation, with the analysis slice defined as the midpoint between the inferior extent of the L3 mask and the superior extent of the L4 mask. The outer body contour on the selected slice was derived by thresholding voxels above −500 Hounsfield units, followed by hole filling, removal of small components, retention of the largest connected component and morphological closing. Outer abdominal circumference was then calculated from the resulting body boundary using DICOM pixel spacing. Adipose tissue was quantified using a hybrid attenuation and AI-based method. Fat voxels were first identified within the body contour using an attenuation range of −195 to −45 Hounsfield units, and these voxels were then partitioned into SAT and VAT using TotalSegmentator tissue-type compartment masks. The outer abdominal circumference (in centimetres), SAT area (in square centimetres), VAT area (in square centimetres) and subcutaneous-to-visceral ratio were exported to an Excel master file. We used this method to quantify WAT distribution parameters in 8,040 patients with BAT from our cohort.

Multi-variable logistic regression analysis revealed that 39 out of 125 individual medications exhibited a significant association with BAT, as depicted in Supplementary Table 6. Of 38 medications examined, 29 had a positive association with the presence of BAT, suggesting that they could act as activators of this tissue. To select candidates for testing, we performed a literature search for each medication focusing on the following criteria: (1) expression of the target receptor and/or pathway in adipocytes, (2) direct effects of the medication in adipocytes and (3) potential cardiometabolic benefits beyond their intended pharmacologic effect. Out of 29 medications, losartan and valsartan fulfilled these criteria.

All animals in this study were male mice on a C57BL/6J background and maintained on a 12-h light–dark cycle with free access to food and water. All mice were housed at 23 °C and fed with a standard rodent chow diet. Animal care and experimentation were performed according to procedures approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees at the Rockefeller University (24019-H) and Weill Cornell Medicine (2017-0043).

For animal studies using losartan, 10- to 14-week-old C57BL/6J wild-type mice were treated with 100 mg kg−1 d−1 losartan by oral gavage twice a day, or losartan dissolved in their drinking water at the same dose. Water intake per mouse was monitored every 48 h to 72 h to warrant the above dose was maintained throughout the experiments. Mice were randomized to treatment, and investigators were not blinded. Sample size was determined based on preliminary experiments. Mice were then euthanized and BAT was collected for protein and RNA analyses.

For assessment of BAT-specific heat production, we used forward-looking infrared (FLIR) thermography using a Teledyne FLIR E96 infrared camera with emissivity set to 0.98. C57BL/6J wild-type mice were treated with 100 mg kg−1 d−1 losartan dissolved in their drinking water for 1 week at room temperature, after which mice were acclimated to thermoneutrality (28 °C) for 1 week before image acquisition. Mice were anaesthetized under isoflurane gas 4 days before experiments, at which point we shaved a 2 × 2-cm region in the midline interscapular and midline lumbar regions. We briefly applied Nair hair removal cream to the shaved regions to ensure lasting skin exposure. Mice were monitored during and after anaesthesia and shaving to ensure full recovery and minimal irritation. After 1 week at thermoneutrality, mice were injected with 1 mg kg−1 of CL-316243 and 2 h later thermal images were captured by gently suspending non-anaesthetized mice by the tail. Duplicate images for each mouse were analysed using FLIR Thermal Studio software. We considered the average maximum temperature recorded in each shaved region as indicators of interscapular and lumbar temperature. The absolute temperature difference between interscapular and lumbar temperature was used to quantify BAT thermogenesis, as reported previously by other researchers67,68,69,70.

Whole-body metabolic measurements were performed using a Promethion Metabolic Screening System for Mice (Sable Systems International). Mice were housed individually with ad libitum access to standard chow diet and given either drinking or losartan water under a 12-h light–dark cycle. Before experimentation, mice were housed singly and acclimatized to room temperature (22 °C) for 10 days within temperature-controlled environmental enclosures (DB034 Laboratory Incubator, Darwin Chambers). For the cold exposure challenge, mice were transferred to a 4 °C environmental chamber and maintained in metabolic cages with a consistent volume of pine chip bedding, without cotton nesting squares for a 5-day experiment. Losartan was administered in drinking water at the indicated concentration throughout the study period, and water intake was monitored continuously. Rates of oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production were acquired by indirect calorimetry with a sampling frequency of 1 s. Respirometry values were determined every 5 min; the dwell time for each cage was 30 s, with baseline cage sampling frequency of 30 s occurring every four cages71. Data were processed further as hourly values and cumulative measurements over 24-h periods, including separate analyses for light and dark phases.

Energy expenditure was assessed using indirect calorimetry and calculated using the Weir equation72. Distance travelled (activity) is determined by x and y position displacements represented by beam breaks within a grid of infrared sensors built into each cage (PedMeters and AllMeters). Food and water intake were recorded automatically. Body weight was measured before, before 4°C exposure and at the end of the study. Animals were monitored daily to ensure health and stability during cold exposure. Raw data were subjected to chamber-level quality control before to analysis. Fat and lean mass were determined using EchoMRI analysis.

Data acquisition and instrument control were coordinated by IM-3 software, and the resulting raw data were processed with Macro Interpreter v.2.38 (Sable Systems) using an analysis script detailing all aspects of data transformation (One-click macro v.2.45; Sable Systems). Indirect calorimetric data were analysed using Excel, R and validated using CalR and graphed using GraphPad Prism v.11.

For RNA extraction from cultured mature adipocytes, cells were lysed in RLT buffer (Qiagen) and RNA was extracted using RNeasy Micro and Mini kits (Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized through reverse transcription using a cDNA synthesis kit (Applied Biosciences). cDNA was analysed by real-time PCR using specific gene primers and a SYBR Green Master Mix (Life Technologies). Relative fold changes of mRNA levels were calculated using the ΔCT method with cyclophilin as loading control. A list of primer sequences can be found in Supplementary Table 10.

For protein extraction from cultured adipocytes and tissue from mice, cells and/or tissue were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer with protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. Protein extracts were resolved on pre-cast polyacrylamide gels (BioRad, 4–20% Mini PROTEAN) and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with appropriate primary antibodies. Detection of proteins was carried out by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies followed by enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagents. Band density was quantified using Fiji/ImageJ (National Institutes of Health (NIH)).

The association between BAT and age, BMI and temperature was assessed by a Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Prevalence of BAT in men and women was compared using a chi-squared test.

To assess whether BAT status was a predictor of cardiometabolic diseases in the PSM cohort, we used multi-variable logistic regression adjusting for age, sex, BMI, ambient temperature, self-reported race and ethnicity, smoking history, family history of cardiovascular disease, cancer site, cancer stage, cancer treatment and general groups of outpatient medications. BAT metabolic activity (total and depot-specific) was calculated as the product of SUV mean and BAT volume (in millilitres) and stratified into tertiles. The association of BAT activity with cardiometabolic diseases was assessed using a multi-variable logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, BMI and outdoor temperature with patients in the BAT− group or those in tertile 1 (lowest BAT metabolic activity) as reference. The correlation of BAT activity with age and outside temperature was calculated with Pearson correlation coefficient after logarithmic transformation. The association of BAT activity with SAT area, VAT area, SAT to VAT ratio and other continuous clinical variables was calculated with linear regression after logarithmic transformation.

The association of BAT status and an expanded set of 511 ICD codes was assessed by univariable logistic regression to identify crude associations. ICD codes associated significantly with BAT were then analysed individually through multi-variable logistic regression analysis adjusting for age, sex, BMI, ambient temperature, self-reported race and ethnicity, smoking history, family history of cardiovascular disease, cancer site, cancer stage, cancer treatment and general groups of outpatient medications. For over-representation analysis, the 511 codes were grouped by organ system. Enrichment for each organ system category was calculated based on the proportion of BAT-associated ICD codes divided by the corresponding proportion among all diagnostic codes in the category set. Statistical significance of over-representation was assessed using Fisher’s exact test and Benjamini–Hochberg FDR was used for multiple test adjustments.

Unpaired t-tests were used to compare laboratory values, imaging studies and clinical indices in patients in the BAT+ versus BAT− groups. Variance equality was assessed with an F-test. When variances were equal, standard t-tests assuming equal variances were used; otherwise, t-tests with unequal variance adjustments were applied. For adjusted analyses, GLMs were used to estimate least square means, adjusting for age, sex, BMI, self-reported race and ethnicity, ambient temperature, cancer site, cancer stage and cancer treatment. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey’s HSD method. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test.

Associations between BAT and individual medications were assessed through multi-variable logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, BMI, temperature, ethnicity, race, smoking history, family history of cardiovascular disease, cancer sites, cancer stage, cancer treatment and cardiovascular diagnoses. For in vitro and in vivo proof-of-concept experiments, group comparisons were conducted using unpaired t-tests. To account for multiple comparisons, P values were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR method, and adjusted P values are reported as such where applicable. Metabolic cage data were analysed, and statistical significance was calculated either using two-way analysis of variance for hourly data or unpaired t-test for 24-h cumulative, light and dark cycle. All analyses were performed using SAS (v.9.4), R software (v.4.5.1) and GraphPad Prism (v.10.5.0). All reported P values are two-sided and statistical significance was defined as α < 0.05.
