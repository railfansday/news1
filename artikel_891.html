Early tetrapod neck mobility modelled using an extant analogue

Tetrapods arose from lobe-finned fishes with a notochord-dominated axial column1,2,3. During this transition, the skull became separated from the shoulder girdle4,5,6, giving rise to a neck region. This head–shoulder separation has generated hypotheses suggesting that neck mobility increased during the origin of tetrapods, facilitating the ecological transition towards more terrestrial habitats4,5,7,8,9. However, incompletely co-ossified vertebral components10 have posed a challenge to modelling the mobility of these early tetrapod necks. Here we used material tests on the similarly configured axial columns of sturgeon to predict the capacities of the tetrapod neck region for lateral flexion, extension and rotation across the tetrapod stem. Our model indicates that the soft tissue-dominated axial column was surprisingly rigid, despite lacking co-ossified vertebral components. We estimate that most stem tetrapods could move their heads less than 6° laterally and less than 3° dorsally but could not rotate them. Notably, mobility did not shift in conjunction with the head–shoulder separation that occurred between the origin of tristichopterids (represented by Eusthenopteron) and that of more crownward stem tetrapods. Therefore, mobility in the tetrapod neck did not serve as a functional driver of head–shoulder separation. Instead, forelimb-driven locomotion, implicated in early tetrapods11,12,13,14, provides a better functional explanation for the head–shoulder separation.

Since the head and trunk first became anatomically distinct in the earliest vertebrates, the interface between these two body regions has undergone a series of evolutionary transformations3,5,9,15. These include a distinct vagal neural crest population16,17,18,19, neck musculature (that is, cucullaris)20,21,22, specialized anterior spinal nerves (the accessory and hypoglossal nerves)23,24,25 and shoulder girdle6,15,26,27. During all these transformations, full head motion (as opposed to intracranial motion) was never independent of trunk motion5,6,9,28,29. However, the skull–trunk linkage underwent a marked change during the origin of tetrapods when the skull and shoulder girdle separated, giving rise to a distinct neck region unique to tetrapods1,5,6. Documented in key stem tetrapods such as Acanthostega4,8 and Tiktaalik9, this head–shoulder separation has given rise to the hypothesis that neck mobility increased markedly through an increase in flexion, extension or rotation across the new neck region during the origin of tetrapods as they began to inhabit more terrestrial habitats4,7,8,9. The predicted increase in neck mobility has not been functionally tested.

Stem tetrapods have an axial column dominated by the soft tissue notochord, which they retain throughout adulthood1,2,3,30,31 (Fig. 1). The notochord is surrounded by distinct cartilaginous or bony arches that lack bone-to-bone joints32,33 (Fig. 1b,c and Extended Data Fig. 1). This condition predates the evolution of the vertebral column characteristic of most adult crown tetrapods in which the notochord is mostly replaced by bony centra. In such an axial column, each vertebral centrum is generally fused to a neural arch, and the vertebrae articulate with one another through inter-central and zygapophyseal joints10. Stem tetrapods lack these stiffening features, despite their hypothesized functional advantage in supporting the axial column for survival in increasingly terrestrial habitats4,7,8,9. These anatomical differences mean that the axial columns of stem tetrapods are not a joint-based system like those of crown tetrapods, making the ‘soft’ axial columns of stem tetrapods challenging to model. No modern tetrapods have an anatomically or functionally comparable axial skeleton10. To generate constrained estimates of the functional capability of the stem tetrapod neck, we sought an alternative model using phylogenetically independent but anatomically comparable extant analogues for which material testing can be performed.

We identified the lake sturgeon (Acipenser fulvescens) as the best available model for the soft axial column of stem tetrapods. Sturgeon retain an intact notochord into adulthood34,35 and have comparable body dimensions to stem tetrapods (Extended Data Figs. 1 and 5). The notochord-dominated axial column of sturgeon, a secondary adaptation in this lineage, has an anatomical configuration similar to that of stem tetrapods in terms of the overall proportions of vertebral arches to the notochordal canal in cross-section (Extended Data Figs. 1 and 2). The arches are joined by unmineralized connective tissues rather than by zygapophyseal joints34,35 and the ventral vertebral components are multipartite (basidorsals and basiventrals in sturgeon; pleurocentra and intercentra in stem and early tetrapods)34. Some anatomical details of sturgeon vertebrae differ from those of their counterparts in stem and early tetrapods. Two examples are that sturgeon have supraneurals and that the basidorsals and basiventrals are placed dorsoventrally across the notochord from one another34, whereas the intercentra and pleurocentra in stem and early tetrapods are staggered relative to one another but both ventrolateral to the notochord (Extended Data Fig. 2)34. Acknowleging these minor differences, we performed a series of material tests on the axial column and notochord of sturgeon specimens and measured lateral flexion stiffness, extension stiffness and torsional (rotational) stiffness. We then applied these stiffness values to the axial column dimensions of stem and early tetrapod taxa to determine the column rigidity and estimate neck mobility in these species.

The mobility of a notochord-dominated axial column is limited by its material properties rather than by zygapophyseal articulations. This type of column is composed of soft tissue (notochord), hard tissue (cartilage or bony arches) and the connective tissues that unite them, all of which contribute to the material properties of the column. Notochord stiffness has been measured in hagfish36, salmon embryos37 and tadpoles38, and its angular stiffness and internal pressure have been calculated in sturgeon during swimming35 (Supplementary Table 3). In hagfish, notochord stiffness alone accounts for most of the body stiffness36. By contrast, reduced ossification of the anterior-most vertebrae in some dragonfish provides the notochordal column with much greater mobility than the rest of the column with ossified centra39; thus, the tissues surrounding the notochord may considerably increase the stiffness of the whole notochordal axial column in non-cyclostomes. To test their relative contributions to body stiffness, we measured the stiffness of both the isolated notochord and the whole column for our range-of-motion estimates of the early tetrapod axial column.

In sturgeon, the whole axial column approximates axial mobility better than does an isolated notochord. Our data indicate that notochord stiffness is not the limiting factor for axial column mobility in sturgeon, as the stiffness of the whole column (with intact mineralized arches and connective tissue) is considerably higher than that of the notochord alone (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 4). This high column stiffness is not attributable to hard stops or joint interactions between the arches, as we did not observe any such interactions or regionalization of bending during testing. Only negligibly small forces and torques were required to bend and twist the notochord compared with the whole column (Extended Data Table 1, Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Fig. 4). Therefore, we used whole-column stiffness to estimate the flexural rigidity of the similarly configured necks of stem tetrapods (Methods).

In lateral flexion, the sturgeon’s posterior precloacal and tail regions of the axial column are less stiff than the anterior-most section (Extended Data Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 5), located just posterior to the fused occipital vertebrae (these are unique to sturgeon in our dataset; Fig. 1). Stiffness was consistent along the column for the other measurements (Extended Data Figs. 3–5 and Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). To account for this variation for sturgeon, we used the Young’s modulus from the anterior-most testing position for lateral flexion (Table 1), as this area is most comparable anatomically to the neck region in stem tetrapods.

To estimate tetrapod neck mobility, we selected five stem tetrapods (Eusthenopteron foordi3,30,31,40, Tiktaalik roseae41, Acanthostega gunnari2, Whatcheeria deltae42 and Greererpeton burkemorani43) and one crown tetrapod (Trimerorhachis insignis44,45,46,47) for which axial column dimensions (Extended Data Fig. 1) are measurable. The vertebral components in these taxa are sufficiently preserved to enable the reconstruction of the anterior-most axial column and the estimation of the body cross-sectional area at the neck. On the basis of the material tests using sturgeon specimens, we estimated that neck mobility is constrained strongly by the axial column in these taxa.

Our modelling revealed only modest neck mobility (Fig. 2) in the tetrapodomorphs Eusthenopteron (lateral flexion of 8.5°) and Tiktaalik (lateral flexion of 5.5°)—the earliest-diverging stem tetrapods in our dataset. Except for Whatcheeria, stem tetrapods were generally not capable of lateral neck flexion of more than 8.5° or neck extension of more than 3°. Among early tetrapods, Acanthostega, Greererpeton and Trimerorachis showed even lower estimates for lateral flexion (<3°) and extension (<1.5°) than their fish outgroups. We generated these estimates for the notochord-dominated anterior-most region of the axial column30,41, where the column is much taller dorsally than it is wide in cross-section (Extended Data Fig. 2c). The column height was at least 166% of the column width in all tetrapods except Tiktaalik and Greererpeton (155% in Tiktaalik; only 128% in Greererpeton). In Eusthenopteron, this height was due to the presence of supraneurals3 (not illustrated in Extended Data Fig. 2a), whereas in other stem and early tetrapods, it was due to the relatively tall neural arches (Extended Data Fig. 2c,d,f). No matter whether height comes from supraneurals or tall neural arches, our estimates generally suggest that taller axial columns have less mobility than do shorter ones.

In a notochord-dominated axial column, the length of the bending region also affects mobility, as the force required to achieve lateral flexion or extension decreases as a cubic function of length (see equation (8); Methods). This inverse cubic function partly explains the low estimates of neck mobility in Acanthostega and Greererpeton and the particularly short-necked Trimerorhachis (1° of lateral flexion and <1° of extension with only three neck vertebrae). Conversely, we attribute the surprisingly high estimated neck mobility in Whatcheeria, in part, to its long neck (Extended Data Fig. 2d). Our modelling suggests that Whatcheeria could achieve up to 32° of lateral flexion and 12° of extension, which exceed our estimates for other early tetrapods by approximately tenfold (lateral flexion) and eightfold (extension). It has a substantially longer neck (89% of the skull length) than other early tetrapods (34.2% of the skull length in Acanthostega and 37.6% in Greererpeton)42. Thus, Whatcheeria seems to be an outlier among early tetrapods in terms of both neck length and mobility; others generally had a highly limited range of head motion.

These high torque estimates suggest that large head turns are infeasible across the tetrapod stem, even in taxa with distinct atlas and axis vertebrae (notably, Greererpeton among the taxa examined here)2,10,42,43,46. We calculated the torques required to produce small 5° head turns in each taxon because the current knowledge of muscular anatomy is insufficient to estimate the maximum rotation achievable by the axial musculature (Extended Data Table 1). Among the taxa examined here, Tiktaalik required the lowest torque (200 Nm) for a 5° head rotation (Extended Data Table 1). This estimated torque is considerable given the limited length of the musculoskeletal elements that form the lever arm (for example, the cervical ribs). All other examined taxa, including Eusthenopteron, would require even higher torque (314–746 Nm) than that required in Tiktaalik for the same degree of rotation (Extended Data Table 1).

Variations in the maximum lateral flexion and extension estimates for stem and early tetrapods are correlated with differences in the body proportions, especially the neck length, of the species analysed. The estimated maximum lateral flexion values of Trimerorhachis, the taxon with the shortest neck (<3 cm, 19% of its skull length; Supplementary Table 5), and Whatcheeria, the taxon with the longest neck (nearly 20 cm, 89% of its skull length; Supplementary Table 5), differ by 31°. However, the overall neck length and the number of vertebrae in the neck region are similar across most stem tetrapods (Eusthenopteron, Tiktaalik, Acanthostega and Greererpeton). Although Eusthenopteron lacks a distinct cervical region defined by the area between the skull and shoulder girdle, an equivalent region can still be identified in the anterior-most axial column by anatomically distinguishable vertebrae (that is, ribless centra or broad supraneurals, depending on the specimen)3,30,40. We measured the axial column length in this region to model theoretical mobility if the region were free (Extended Data Table 2 and Extended Data Fig. 2). The lateral flexion capacities of the axial columns of Eusthenopteron and Tiktaalik exceed those of Acanthostega and Greererpeton by two- to threefold (Fig. 2 and Extended Data Table 2), despite comparable neck lengths. These differences seem to result from variations in the geometries of both the axial column and body cross-sections (Extended Data Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3).

Axial column geometry influences the moment of inertia (I in equations (8) and (9); Methods), whereas the body cross-sectional area limits the maximum muscle area (Methods and Supplementary Fig. 3). The ratio of vertebral width to height affects I and, therefore, the column mobility, especially in extension. In Tiktaalik, the vertebrae are nearly as tall as they are wide at the base and this enables 2.25° of extension (Extended Data Figs. 1c,e and 2b; similar to Greererpeton, Extended Data Fig. 2e). Vertebral height is greater in other taxa (up to 2.17 times the width in Acanthostega; Extended Data Table 1 and Extended Data Fig. 2c) and extension is correspondingly lower (0.6° in Acanthostega; Fig. 2 and Extended Data Table 2). To further assess how vertebral proportions affect bending, we calculated bending for a theoretical 10 cm-long neck with the column of each taxon scaled to the same width. We found not only that tall and narrow vertebral proportions are indeed restrictive, especially in Acanthostega, but also that the rectangular profile of Trimerorhachis strongly restricts bending even in a long axial column section, so its low head mobility is only partly due to its very short neck (Supplementary Table 9).

Crownward tetrapods tend to have a relatively wide axial column, as seen in Greererpeton (Extended Data Fig. 2e). This is due in part to robust transverse processes33—a geometry that is favourable for greater mobility (Supplementary Table 9). In other taxa, such as embolomeres and amniotes, this widening is also correlated with an increasing replacement of the notochord by vertebral centra. Our current method is inappropriate for estimating the range of mobility in taxa such as these, with restricted notochords. The zygapophyseal connections between vertebrae are also elaborated in crown tetrapods, adding complex joints to the vertebral column. Evaluating early tetrapod and early amniote axial columns with more developed zygapophyseal articulations will require a different approach.

Our estimates of lateral flexion, extension and rotational capacities are based on the flexural and torsional rigidity of the axial column. In a whole body, additional tissues superficial to the axial column—namely the integument, muscles, osteoderms and ribs—also contribute considerable stiffness48. Further constraints on tetrapod neck mobility could also arise from internal organs that cannot tolerate the extension or compression caused by head motion, such as the gill apparatus in, for example, Eusthenopteron40, Tiktaalik49 and Acanthostega50.

Our models of neck mobility across stem and early tetrapods contradict the predicted increase in neck mobility driven by head–shoulder separation4,7,8,9. Instead, they suggests that the separation event (which occurred among the common ancestors shared by Tiktaalik and Acanthostega but was not shared with Eusthenopteron9,49) had no detectable effect on neck mobility (Fig. 2). Overall, stem tetrapods had stiffer necks than might be expected given their soft tissue-dominated axial columns (the properties assessed are summarized in Supplementary Table 3). In these taxa, the capacities for lateral flexion and extension were limited to only a few degrees. Furthermore, the capacity for head rotation was absent or, at best, extremely limited in stem and early tetrapods. In exceptional cases among stem tetrapods (such as Whatcheeria) and derived crown tetrapods, the neck enables substantial and independent head motion; however, this ability seems to have evolved secondarily to the origin of the tetrapod neck (that is, head–shoulder separation).

Our results offer an alternative to the prevailing interpretation4,5,7,8,9,51 that the separation of the skull and pectoral girdle indicates independent neck mobility through a free neck in stem tetrapods. A stiff axial column, as inferred from our analysis, is consistent with the amphibious lifestyle hypothesized for most stem tetrapods4,7,8,51, in which low neck mobility may have been advantageous for locomotion in water by increasing energetic efficiency during swimming52. A stiff axial column may also have contributed to passive resistance to gravity in terrestrial environments. Thus, given the limited neck mobility in stem tetrapods, alternative functional explanations for the head–shoulder separation are warranted.

We argue that the head–shoulder separation is more consistent with the predicted origin of forelimb-driven locomotion in early tetrapods4,11,12,13,14. The pectoral fin and girdle underwent several anatomical changes across the tetrapod stem8,12, including the rearrangement of the long bones and the loss of lepidotrichia13,52,53,54. Eusthenopteron, the earliest-branching tetrapodomorph considered, already has a stress-dissipating trabecular architecture in its humerus that could support weight bearing14. More derived stem tetrapods such as Ichthyostega are hypothesized to have used pectoral-powered locomotion in contact with the substrate11,42,51,55,56, similar to the terrestrial locomotion observed in several extant fishes13,57,58. These lines of inference reveal a trend of increasing demand on the shoulder girdle to support forelimb function4,9,11,27,58 rather than to connect the head with the trunk5,6,15 across the tetrapod stem. Thus, the head–shoulder separation in stem tetrapods probably resulted from this shift in primary shoulder function.

In fact, the tetrapod neck seems to have evolved as a by-product of the head–shoulder separation, rather than as an adaptation to increase neck mobility. Although the axial column remained generally stiff in stem tetrapods, derived tetrapods acquired greater neck mobility, partly by having longer necks and partly through the evolution of mobile intervertebral joints59 that enable lateral flexion, extension60 and rotation61. The delayed acquisition of neck mobility calls into question the relevance of some hypothesized terrestrial feeding modes for early tetrapods51, because such limited head mobility would preclude lateral snapping when the forelimb was planted except, perhaps, in Whatcheeria42. Meanwhile, our results align with hypotheses regarding aquatic feeding behaviour that suggest that additional lateral flexion in the trunk contributes to overall motion51,62 and broadly support amphibious locomotory habits among stem and early tetrapods11,42,51,56.

Ten A. fulvescens yearlings were partially dissected for material characterization. This number was not predetermined statistically. The fish were housed and cared for prior to being provided to us dead in accordance with the animal use protocol (22003) approved by the Canadian Council on Animal Care and Wilfrid Laurier University’s Animal Care Committee. Additional specimens from the same source, as well as waste from commercially fished sturgeon, were used for test design and figures (Extended Data Table 3).

The tested specimens were thawed and their axial columns were dissected out in the morning on the day of testing (Extended Data Fig. 1a, Supplementary Methods, Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Each dissected axial column included a cube of bone and cartilage from the posterior-most section of the skull and the caudal fin to avoid cutting the notochord at any point along its length and compromising its mechanical properties. Dissected specimens were kept moist and stored on ice between dissection and testing and between trials. The column was dissected further on site for torsion testing of specific sections and for testing the notochord (Supplementary Methods). Tissue bending was measured on a ZwickiLine Z5.0 TN materials testing machine (ZwickRoell) using a ZwickRoell GTM series K load cell (5 kN capacity, International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 7500-1 accuracy class 1 from 20 N to 5 kN). Measurement electronics provide a 24 bit resolution across the range corresponding to a force resolution finer than 0.01 N; although our maximum loads fell below the threshold of the ISO, they remained well within the electronic resolution of the system and were repeatable across measurements. Torque was measured using a 20 Nm torque transducer (ISO 7500-1 accuracy class 1 from 0.2 Nm to 20 Nm) on the same machine. The full-scale range corresponds to a resolution finer than 0.0001 Nm; although some of our measured torques fell below the ISO threshold, they also remained well within the electronic resolution of the system. The first round of manipulation in each trial is the source of data in this study, with additional rounds being performed in case these data are of interest for future materials assessments.

First, three-point lateral bending (equivalent to lateral flexion) was performed with the supporting pins set at a 4 cm span and the loading pin set to a 5 mm depression depth (five rounds of depression per trial). Next, dorsoventral bending (equivalent to extension) was conducted with the same span but with the depression depth decreased to 3 mm to avoid bending the tissue to a damaging degree.

After the bending tests, the column was dissected further into sections between every 12th rib and the subsequent rib. The skull cube was not removed to avoid cutting the anterior end of the notochord. Each section was clamped vertically in the machine with the anterior part facing upwards. The torsion drive was then rotated 30° clockwise, held for 1 s and returned to the starting position three times per trial.

The notochord was dissected from each column. Lateral bending and dorsoventral bending were performed consequently with the supporting pins set at a 2 cm span and the loading pin set to a 3 mm depression depth (five rounds per trial). Small pieces of coarse sandpaper (60–100 grit) were placed between the sample and support points to stabilize the specimen when it rolled out of the desired orientation during set-up. We ensured that the added sandpaper did not overlap with the span between the supporting pins so as to avoid increasing the stiffness of the notochord area that was being tested.

Testing trials for specimens 2401-01 and 2401-02 followed the same protocol as for whole-column torsion measurements. During initial trials, we observed a sinusoidal curve indicative of viscoelastic properties. We therefore increased the hold time to 3 s at 30° for specimens 2401-04–2401-10.

We measured the axial column and body cross-sectional area in computed tomography scans of the original specimens using Materialise Mimics (v.26.0).
