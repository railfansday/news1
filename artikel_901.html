The epidermis is a stratified epithelium comprising proliferative basal cells and differentiated suprabasal cells that are continuously renewed throughout life. Conflicting theories have been proposed regarding the heterogeneity of the basal cell layer, with some studies proposing that all proliferative cells have self-renewal potential during homeostasis1,2,3,4,5. Meanwhile, others posit a model in which stem cell differentiation occurs through an intermediate transit-amplifying-like progenitor state with strictly limited proliferative potential6,7,8,9,10. Here, to determine the heterogeneity and pattern of cell fate, we performed intravital time-lapse imaging to track the fate of lineage-labelled cells in the skin interfollicular epidermis. Results from quantitative analyses of clonal dynamics support a two-compartment model of epidermal homeostasis, whereby basal cells are organized in a stem cell–transit-amplifying cell hierarchy. Critical to these conclusions was the discovery of sexual dimorphism in epidermal fate, with female mice showing a highly synchronized pattern of cell division, differentiation and delamination that contributes to accelerated wound healing. Single-cell RNA sequencing, transcriptional profiling and clonal analyses of castrated mice revealed that male sex hormones promote stem cell differentiation and delamination. In summary, our study provides insight into the functional identity of the stem cell population and identifies sex-specific differences in the regulation of epidermal stem cells and transit-amplifying cell fate.

The skin epidermis is a compartmentalized tissue that is subdivided into the hair follicle, interfollicular epidermis (IFE), infundibulum and sebaceous gland. All of these cell types are maintained by distinct populations of stem cells (SCs)11,12. The IFE is a stratified epithelium comprising a proliferative basal layer and multiple layers of differentiated suprabasal cells11. Following terminal differentiation, post-mitotic basal cells delaminate into the suprabasal cell compartment before shedding from the skin surface11.

Several models have been proposed to explain the cellular mechanisms that maintain homeostasis of squamous epithelia12. From studies of rats, it was initially proposed that oesophageal epithelium is maintained by a single equipotent population in which cell loss through differentiation is compensated by the duplication of neighbours13. Subsequently, on the basis of histological and proliferation kinetic analyses of skin IFE in mice, others proposed that the IFE comprises a mosaic of epidermal proliferative units in which individual slow-cycling SCs support a pool of transit-amplifying (TA) cells and their differentiated progeny14. However, clonal lineage tracing challenged the idea of epidermal proliferative units. Instead, it was suggested that the IFE is maintained by a single proliferative compartment in which most (around 80%) of divisions result in an asymmetric fate outcome. The remaining compartment leads to balanced stochastic SC loss through differentiation or replacement through duplication1,2,3. Further short-term intravital imaging of the epidermis supported a single proliferative compartment model, with spatiotemporal coordination between sister cells4 and local coordination of cell division and loss5.

However, several observations have challenged the one-compartment model. First, lineage tracing revealed differences in the long-term survival and growth potential of clonal populations using CreER lines linked to the expression of different targeted promoters6,7,8,9,10. Second, fate-mapping studies of back skin epidermis under different experimental conditions showed evidence of an enrichment of clones with even cell numbers9. This feature is difficult to explain in the framework of a one-compartment model. Third, fate mapping of the ear skin epidermis based on a keratin 10 (Krt10) promoter showed evidence of proliferative cells with limited proliferative potential. This result suggests that subsequent commitment to differentiation cells maintains the capacity to divide, which requires multiple proliferative populations or a continuum of SC states15. Finally, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of both mouse9,15,16,17,18,19,20,21,22 and human tissue23,24 revealed heterogeneity in the basal cell population. However, the functional identity and significance of transcriptional states remained unclear.

Besides reproductive organs, the sex of an animal has been associated with differences in organ morphology and tissue composition25,26,27. In skin, morphological differences between male and female animals have been reported28,29, including impacts of hormones on wound healing30,31,32. However, little is known about the role of sex or sex hormones on epidermal SC fate in homeostasis and repair.

To determine the functional identity of cell populations in the skin IFE, emphasis has been placed on clonal data obtained from fixed samples, in which temporal information on cell lineages can only be inferred indirectly. Here we examine the fate of individual lineage-labelled cells and their progeny in the ear skin epidermis by tracking the dynamics of clones in time lapse over weeks to months. By combining single-cell gene expression profiling with biophysical modelling, we show that IFE maintenance relies on a locally coordinated pattern of stochastic SC renewal in which commitment to differentiation is not direct but occurs through an intermediate TA-cell-like state with limited proliferative potential. Furthermore, detailed features of clonal dynamics reveal temporal coordination of sister cell events that is strongly enriched in female mice. Functional experiments link this coordination with the role of sex hormones in the regulation of basal cell dynamics.

To determine the repertoire of proliferative cell states, their lineage relationships and pattern of fate in the ear skin IFE, we used intravital microscopy to quantify the size, composition and spatial organization of clones over 5 months of time-lapse imaging. Low-dose tamoxifen administration to Krt14creER;iChr-control-mosaic mice induced stochastic expression of one of three nuclear fluorescent reporters (mCherry, GFP or Cerulean), which were sufficiently spaced to enable unambiguous clone tracking by time-lapse imaging. By recording the spatial coordinates of clones, their dynamics could be monitored in a precise manner over weeks and months. Notably, it was possible to score the frequencies of events leading to clone loss (‘extinction’). This process enabled the discrimination of clones that become lost after one or two divisions from those that expand further before incidental loss (Fig. 1a–d and Extended Data Fig. 1a–c).

To generate a statistically meaningful dataset, we tracked the temporal evolution of 1,171 clones from 10 mice (5 females and 5 males) over a 140-day time course, with time points extending from once a week to once every 2 weeks then once a month (Fig. 1a–d and Supplementary Fig. 5a–j). After combining clonal data from all animals, irrespective of sex, the mean basal cell number of surviving clones steadily increased from 1.47 ± 0.03 cells at day 7 (D7) to 6.5 ± 0.6 cells at D84. By contrast, the clonal persistence—the probability that a clone retains at least one basal cell—decreased to 27 ± 2% at D84 (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). Notably, the majority of clones visible at D7 (58 ± 4%, n = 653) did not expand beyond 3 cells over the 5-month time course. Of those that did expand beyond this threshold, around 23 ± 3% (n = 292) were subsequently lost through differentiation over the time course, whereas 19 ± 2% (n = 226) were still present at the final time point (Fig. 1d). The average labelled cell density—the product of mean clone size and clonal persistence—rapidly converged to a constant value, which indicated that Krt14creER targets renewing cells in a representative manner33 (Extended Data Fig. 2c).

After grouping by sex, we observed differences in the average properties of clones between male and female mice, with the average basal clone size consistently higher in female mice (Fig. 1e–g and Supplementary Note). Moreover, the frequency of clones that stayed within a maximum of 3 cells was higher in male mice (65 ± 3%, n = 268) than in female mice (52 ± 6%, n = 437; z-test z = 3.4, P < 0.001). Both the basal and suprabasal cell number of clones showed a marked enrichment in even sizes in female mice. This enrichment persisted throughout the entire 5-month time course and was apparent even among the largest clones (Fig. 1h,k and Extended Data Fig. 2d–f). A similar enrichment pattern was observed in the back and tail skin IFE of female mice at D28 after labelling. This finding indicates that this pattern is a general feature of epidermal cells (Extended Data Fig. 2i–m).

To further validate these findings, we performed unbiased short-term lineage tracing using 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) pulse–chase experiments. EdU is incorporated during S phase and diluted through subsequent rounds of division, which enables fate mapping of dividing cells. After a 6-day chase, the pseudo-clone size distribution revealed a clear enrichment of even-sized basal cell numbers, a result that mirrored the long-term time-lapse imaging results (Extended Data Fig. 2g,h).

To determine the functional heterogeneity and fate behaviour of basal cells, we questioned the origin of the persistent and robust even-odd signature of clone sizes and why the strength might differ between male and female animals. In anticipation of the role of local correlations in the coordination of cell division and delamination events, we considered a minimal spatial model of the basal layer based on a 2D Voronoi-type framework34 (for details, see Supplementary Note and ref. 22). Noting the cohesive nature of clones, we positioned the model in a solid-like regime. Here in the absence of turnover, cells are jammed so that neighbour exchange is suppressed35.

To model the clonal data, we first considered a one-proliferative compartment model. To ensure homeostasis of the SC density, the frequency of symmetric divisions was subject to mechanical feedback based on an area-based rule. That is, when the local cell density (as indexed through the cell area) is low, SC divisions are tilted towards duplication. Conversly, when the cell density is high, SC divisions are biased towards terminal differentiation. Notably, although this model could provide a satisfactory fit to the observed dependencies on the average clone size, the hallmark even-odd clone-size correlations in female mice could not be reproduced. To capture the abrupt increase followed by the shallow linear-like growth in the average basal clone size (Fig. 1e), the frequency of SC divisions that result in asymmetric fate must be high (around 70–90%, as reported in other squamous tissues)1,3,6. However, under such conditions, it was not possible to reproduce the scale of the observed enhancement of even clone sizes in both the basal and suprabasal compartments. Furthermore, the model could not capture key features of the data, including the enrichment of even-sized jumps in clone size across consecutive time points (Extended Data Fig. 3a–l and Supplementary Note).

On the basis of these findings, we considered a two-proliferative compartment model, in which SC differentiation occurs through an intermediate TA-cell-like state (Fig. 1l and Supplementary Videos 1 and 2). Placing SC fate under the same mechanical feedback control, we proposed that SC division can also lead to three possible fate outcomes. However, in this case, TA cells are generated. TA cell division then leads to either terminal division or asymmetric fate outcome, which then gives rise to terminally differentiated basal cells. Finally, to capture even clone-size correlations (Extended Data Fig. 2e), we further proposed that the timing of sister SC and TA cell division and the timing of sister cell delaminations are correlated (Supplementary Note).

To fit the two-compartment model, we first took advantage of the temporal nature of the data to constrain model parameters. To that end, we segregated lineages into the following types of clones: clones comprising a maximum of a single cell (D-like), which represent cells induced in the differentiated basal cell compartment; clones that grow to a maximum of three cells (TA-like), which are enriched in clones derived from the putative TA cell compartment; and the remaining (SC-like) clones, which are derived from the putative SC compartment. Measurements of the average size of SC-like clones over the short term provided an estimate of the cell proliferation rate. By contrast, the long-term labelled cell densities provided an estimate of the SC fraction in the basal layer and the ratio of cell loss to proliferation rate. With these estimates in hand, a parameter scan showed that the two-proliferative compartment model could reproduce the clone-size dependencies of both male and female mice. These dependencies included the persistent even-odd clone size correlations and the transition probabilities of clone sizes between consecutive time points (Fig. 1i–n, Extended Data Fig. 4a–i and Supplementary Note).

We then questioned the origin of the differences observed between male and female animals. Notably, the average residency time of terminally differentiated basal cells was predicted to be shorter in male mice (3.0 ± 0.4 days) than in female mice (5.1 ± 0.3 days). By contrast, the frequency of terminal TA cell divisions was higher in female mice (>95%) than in male mice (70%). Moreover, the delamination of differentiated sister cells was highly coordinated for female mice, with a correlation of sister cell lifetimes of about 0.9 compared with about 0.6 in male mice (Supplementary Note).

These findings point to a spatially and temporally coordinated pattern of turnover in the ear skin IFE, in which most SC divisions result in an asymmetric fate outcome and the majority of TA cell divisions result in symmetric differentiation. The temporal coordination of sister cell divisions and delaminations leads predominantly to a local turnover of SC–TA cell pairs, which results in the observed long-term enhancement of even clone sizes.

To validate the model predictions, we closely monitored cell dynamics. To that end, we imaged clones every 12 h for 5 days and we recorded division and delamination events to reconstruct lineage trees and to assess correlations between sister cell events. Estimates of correlation coefficients revealed a strong temporal correlation between sister cell lifetimes. We calculated correlation coefficients between sister events, with a coefficient of 0 indicating no correlation, and 1 indicating events that occur in the same 12-h time window. In male mice, we observed coefficients of 0.89 ± 0.03 between delaminating sisters, 0.70 ± 0.15 between dividing sisters and 0.75 ± 0.18 when one sister divided and one sister delaminated. In female mice, the corresponding coefficients were 0.82 ± 0.14, 0.8 ± 0.12 and 0.3 ± 0.3, respectively (although the latter events were rare). Within a 12-h time window, 84% (42 out of 50) delaminations in male clones and 98% (45 out of 46) of delaminations in female clones involved sister cells. Moreover, the relative transition probabilities between clone sizes across consecutive time points were similar in short-term and long-term imaging data. Repeated daily imaging slightly increased cell division rates compared with the less invasive twice a month imaging schedule used for long-term clonal analyses and biophysical modelling. Overall, short-term imaging confirmed the tight temporal coordination predicted by the model to occur between sister cells (Extended Data Fig. 5a–d and Supplementary Note).

We next investigated whether sex-specific differences in cell fate affect wound-healing kinetics. After a wound was made on the back skin, the kinetics of wound closure was significantly faster in female mice than in male mice. To determine whether this dimorphism was the consequence of different responses of keratinocytes, we assessed proliferation rates using EdU and clone fate mapping. As we and others have shown36,37, keratinocyte responses to wound healing are divided into two areas. The first is a non-proliferative leading edge consisting of only migratory cells and the second is a proliferative ring, where cells actively divide to mediate wound closure. At D2 after wounding, a 4-h EdU pulse revealed a thicker proliferative ring in female than in male mice (800 ± 37 µm and 590 ± 60 µm, respectively) and a significantly higher density of proliferative cells (4,330 ± 320 and 2,860 ± 330 EdU cells per mm2, respectively). By D4, this sex difference was markedly reduced (Fig. 2a–f and Supplementary Fig. 6a,b). Consistent with this difference in proliferation kinetics, lineage tracing showed that at D2, basal clone size was significantly larger in female than male mice (4.7 ± 0.2 and 3.0 ± 0.1 cells, respectively), a difference still present, albeit decreased, at D4. This difference was reflected in the enrichment of even clone sizes in female mice at D4 after wounding compared with males (Fig. 2g–h). These findings indicate that wound healing promotes a higher degree of cell coordination in female than in male mice. Together with more rapid SC and/or TA cell activation in the initial stage of wound healing, this process accelerates wound closure.

To test whether male sex hormones drive this sexual dimorphism, we castrated male mice and assessed delamination rates via short-term EdU pulse–chase. After castration, delamination rates were similar to female mice (21 ± 3% in female mice, 14 ± 2% in castrated male mice and 47 ± 6% in male control mice for doublets with one delaminated cell). The asymmetric division rate (estimated as EdU doublets with one KRT14+ and one KRT10+ cell) was higher in male (74 ± 4%) than in female (60 ± 3%) mice and decreased after castration (53 ± 2%). Ovariectomy in female mice did not lead to differences in basal cell delamination rates or cell fate outcomes (Fig. 3b,c).

To assess whether Y chromosome genes, beyond male sex hormones, affect epidermal cell differentiation and delamination, we used the four core genotype (FCG) mouse model38. This model involves deletion of the testis-determining gene Sry from the Y chromosome and introducing an autosomal Sry transgene. Consequently, gonadal sex depends on the transgene rather than the Y chromosome. After 96 h of EdU pulse–chase, Sry-expressing mice, irrespective of their chromosomal status, showed a significantly increased delamination rate (17 ± 2% for Sry+ versus 8 ± 1% for Sry–). XX and XY animals did not show significant delamination differences (13 ± 2% and 11 ± 3%, respectively). Similar data were observed for fate outcome of dividing cells (KRT14+KRT10+ EdU doublets of 60 ± 3% for Sry+ versus 42 ± 4% for Sry–) (Fig. 3d,e). To further clarify the role of male sex hormones, we assessed the impact of hormone replacement using dihydrotestosterone (DHT), a biologically active form of testosterone, in castrated male mice. We confirmed that androgen receptors are expressed by IFE basal cells in these mice. DHT administration increased basal cell delamination at 96 h (10 ± 1% for castrated mice compared with 31 ± 4% for DHT-treated mice) and similarly affected dividing cell fate outcome (KRT14+KRT10+ EdU doublets of 41 ± 4% for castrated mice versus 58 ± 3% for DHT-treated mice) (Fig. 3f–h). Together, these data show that testosterone promotes epidermal differentiation and basal cell delamination.

To confirm that male sex hormones influence clonal dynamics of epidermal SC and TA cells, we performed long-term lineage tracing in castrated male mice. Mean basal clone size, clone persistence and labelled cell fractions were similar between control and castrated mice. However, clones from castrated mice showed an enrichment of even cell numbers compared with non-castrated male mice. Fitting of the two-proliferative compartment model to the clonal data independently confirmed that male sex hormones affect basal cell delamination and asymmetric TA cell division frequency (Fig. 3i and Extended Data Fig. 6a–e,k), which led to the restoration of female-like features. Together, these data demonstrate that male hormones influence epidermal SC and TA cell clonal dynamics to enhance the rate of cell delamination following terminal differentiation.

To better understand how keratinocyte proliferation is controlled in male and female mice, we performed 4-h EdU pulse experiments during homeostasis. In the ear skin IFE, female mice presented a higher degree of proliferation than male mice (7.2 ± 0.3% and 5.6 ± 0.4%, respectively). Modulation of male sex hormones (castration then with or without DHT treatment) did not influence proliferation. However, IFE basal cells expressed oestrogen receptors, and modulation of female sex hormones by ovariectomy decreased basal cell proliferation (7.2 ± 0.3% for control and 4.9 ± 0.3% after ovariectomy). Oestradiol administration after ovariectomy restored the proliferation rate in female mice (7.2 ± 0.3% for control and 7.6 ± 0.7% after oestradiol treatment; Fig. 3j–l). Finally, to assess how female sex hormones affect cell coordination, we performed long-term lineage tracing in ovariectomized mice. Absence of female sex hormones led to a near-total disappearance of even basal clone size enrichment (Fig. 3m and Extended Data Fig. 6f–k). This result suggests that regulation of proliferation by oestrogen affects the female-specific increase in basal cell coordination and the degree of asymmetric TA cell division (Supplementary Note).

To further characterize ear skin IFE heterogeneity and the molecular drivers of sexual dimorphism, we performed scRNA-seq of FACS-isolated basal cells from adult male and female mice. As previously reported16,17,22, three G0/G1 clusters were identified (Methods, Extended Data Fig. 7a–j and Supplementary Fig. 7a–e). To investigate basal cell lineage specification, we visualized cell trajectories on a three-dimensional (3D) manifold based on a diffusion map. The 3D manifold preserved the continuity of cell cycle progression, which enabled us to interpret how different G0/G1 populations are connected to proliferative states while maintaining global organization of the differentiation manifold. Unsupervised clustering revealed three non-cell-cycle-related clusters that echoed the three G0/G1 clusters—SC, TA and differentiated cells—identified through clonal fate mapping. The SC cluster preferentially expressed SC-related genes39,40,41, such as Lgr6, Trp73, Sox6 and Jag2, whereas the TA cluster was defined by high levels of basal keratins (Krt5 and Krt14), Wnt4, S100a6, Actb and Fth1. The differentiated cluster expressed differentiation markers such as Krt10, Grhl1, Notch1 and Krtdap (Fig. 4a–d and Extended Data Fig. 7k,n–p). Cells from both the SC and TA cell clusters were able to enter the cell cycle loop, whereas most of the differentiated cells were post-mitotic. RNA velocity analysis indicated a developmental progression from the SC to the TA cell cluster, followed by cell cycle exit and differentiation. SCENIC analysis of activated transcription factor (TF) regulons revealed enrichment of the Trp73 regulon in SCs, Klf4 in TA cells and Grhl1, Gata3 and Klf4 among differentiated cells (Fig. 4e–g and Extended Data Fig. 8a–f).

To link the clonal fate mapping, modelling and scRNA-seq results, we performed lineage tracing using different CreER mouse models based on the following putative SC and TA cell marker genes: Lgr6creER for SC and Notch1creER for TA and differentiated cells. To capture early fate decisions and clonal loss in TA and differentiated cells, we started imaging at D3 instead of D7. After combining clonal data from all animals, we observed a substantial difference in D28 clonal persistence between Lgr6creER (44.6 ± 4.0%) and Notch1creER (10.0 ± 0.3%). This finding indicated that Lgr6creER (SC-enriched) and Notch1creER (TA and differentiated-enriched) label functionally discrete basal populations. To assess whether these targeted cells were rooted in self-renewing populations, we looked at the change in average labelled cell number. Notably, an early decrease in labelled cell number in the Notch1creER-targeted population indicated that initially labelled proliferative cells were not enriched in cells with self-renewal potential. This result is consistent with the identity of a putative TA cell population. Subsequently, the labelled cell density stabilized, which is most likely to be caused by residual SC labelling by Notch1creER. By contrast, the Lgr6creER-targeted population showed an early increase in average labelled cell number, followed by a convergence to a constant value, a result consistent with the labelling of SCs (Fig. 4h–m).

When following a single clone over time, 75.5 ± 1.0% of clones did not show division in Notch1creER tracing, whereas in Lgr6creER tracing, non-dividing clones represented only 18.7 ± 5.1% of the total. Of those clones that did divide, the frequency of clones that stayed within the maximum 3-cell threshold (putative TA cells) was 62.1 ± 3.7% in Notch1creER-targeted clones and 38.6 ± 5.7% in Lgr6creER-targeted clones (Fig. 4n–o). These findings were consistent with the fact that Notch1creER-labelled cells mainly comprised terminally differentiated and TA cells compared with Lgr6creER-labelled cells. By segregating clones into SC-like, TA-like and D-like populations, Notch1 and Lgr6 clones were consistent with our two-proliferative compartment model (Extended Data Figs. 9a–j and 10a–n and Supplementary Note).

The fraction of labelled basal cells expressing KRT10 at D3 was 7.7 ± 2.9% in Lgr6creER-targeted cells from male mice and 9.2 ± 1.0% from female mice. By contrast, in Notch1creER-targeted cells, the proportion was 38.2 ± 4.5% in male mice and 55.3 ± 3.8% in female mice. The proportion of Lgr6creER-labelled cells that were suprabasal was 0% in male mice and 1.4 ± 1.4% in female mice, compared with 27.4 ± 4.4% of Notch1creER-labelled cells in male mice and 21.3 ± 1.4% in female mice (Extended Data Fig. 9k–n). Altogether, these findings support the existence of two proliferative basal cell compartments, with SCs preferentially targeted by Lgr6creER and TA and differentiated cells preferentially targeted by Notch1creER.

To define the molecular mechanisms that underlie sex-specific differences in epidermal fate, we assessed the most differentially expressed genes between male and female mice across the different clusters. Besides Y-expressed genes, the IFE SC cluster from male mice showed increased levels of Skint1 and differentiation-related genes such as Ehf and Krtdap. The TA cell cluster from male mice showed increased levels of differentiation markers such as Krtdap or Skint1. Similarly, differentiated basal cells had increased expression of the differentiation marker Krtdap. By contrast, female mice showed elevated expression of SC marker genes such as Postn and Smoc2, as well as specific expression of immune-related genes such as Il34 and Il20ra. In line with these results, SCENIC analysis identified sex-based enrichment of potential TF regulon activation. Among SC, TA and differentiated clusters, the well-established TF associated with epidermal differentiation, Klf4 (ref. 42), was enriched in basal cells from male compared with female mice (Extended Data Figs. 7l,m and 8g–l and Supplementary Data 1–3).

To investigate in more detail the transcriptional differences mediated by sex hormones, we performed bulk RNA-seq on FACS-isolated basal cells from male and female mice and castrated male mice. As sex hormone regulation of epidermal cells could occur indirectly, we assessed whether castration influences other skin parameters. Dermal thickness was unchanged after castration. Hair follicle cycle interference was limited as—in the ear epidermis—hair follicles do not cycle after morphogenesis43 and castration did not alter their resting stages (Extended Data Fig. 11a–d). Bulk RNA-seq is more sensitive than scRNA-seq in detecting differences in RNA expression levels, with a higher signal-to-noise ratio. However, it has the disadvantage of reflecting the average composition of the different cell states present in the basal layer at any given time. Nevertheless, similar to the scRNA-seq data, the most differentially expressed genes were linked to sex chromosomes. Some previously described human sex-specific genes were identified. Notably, osteoglycin (Ogn), a proteoglycan previously reported to be more highly expressed in male than in female mice44, was differentially expressed in epidermal basal cells between male and female mice. Several extracellular matrix (ECM) genes such as proteoglycan glypican 2 (Gpc2), collagens and collagen-modifying proteins, basal lamina-specific proteins such as Lamb1 and actin regulators such as Ablim3 and Fhl1, were expressed at higher levels in epidermal cells in male than in female mice (Fig. 5a and Extended Data Fig. 12a,b,e–j).

By contrast, many differentiation markers were upregulated in basal cells in female compared with male mice. Genes associated with terminal differentiation of keratinocytes, including Krt1, involucrin (Ivl), filaggrin (Flg), the transglutaminase Tgm1, the TF Ovol1 and cornifins (Sprr2a1, Sprr2a2, Sprr1a, Sprr1b), were all expressed at higher levels in females. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of sex-specific differentially expressed genes, using commitment-associated genes from epidermal basal cells in vivo6, confirmed that basal cells from female mice are associated with more epidermal differentiation genes. Notably, many of these differentiation genes were upregulated in male mice after castration (Fig. 5b,c and Extended Data Fig. 12e–m). Note that the apparent difference between the scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data reflects the differences in the relative proportion of SC, TA and differentiated cells in the basal layer between male and female mice owing to the faster cell delamination rate in males, which occurs in an androgen-regulated manner. To further test the enrichment of terminally differentiated basal cells in female mice, we assessed KRT14 and KRT10 expression in the basal layer. The frequency of epidermal cells expressing KRT10 but not KRT14 was increased in females, a finding consistent with an elevated proportion of differentiated basal cells (Fig. 5d–g). Consistent with results from the EdU pulse experiments (Fig. 3k), female mice showed increased expression of proliferation-associated genes, such as Aspm, Birc5 and Top2a (Fig. 5b), which indicated that sexual dimorphism affects both differentiation and proliferation. Moreover, some immune-related genes were expressed in a sexually dimorphic manner and modulated by castration (Fig. 5a,b).

To define how male sex hormones regulate sex-specific transcriptional programs, we assessed how genes differentially expressed between the sexes were regulated after castration. About 10% (45 out of 434) of genes preferentially expressed in male mice were downregulated following castration. Several sex dimorphic ECM components (Ogn, Gpc2, Clu, Egfl6 and Lamb1), actin and immunity-related genes (Ablim3, Fhl1, Cxcl12 and Ccl27b) were downregulated after castration (Extended Data Fig. 12d). This result reflects the fact that regulation of such genes is mediated by testosterone.

Almost 50% of the genes upregulated following castration were upregulated in female mice, which suggests that male sex hormones negatively regulate genes expressed at increased levels in female mice (Extended Data Fig. 12c,k–m). Most of the keratinization genes (Flg, Tgm1, Ivl, Dsc2 and Lce1g), which reflect the proportion of differentiated cells in the basal layer, were upregulated after castration, consistent with the decreased delamination rate observed following male castration (Fig. 3b).

To assess the human relevance of our analysis, we studied the impact of male sex hormones on the differentiation and stratification of human epidermal cells using in vitro 3D skin equivalent (3DSE) keratinocytes45. Primary human keratinocytes from male and female donors were seeded on 3T3-J2 feeder cells mixed with collagen gels. Human keratinocytes were cultured in the presence or absence of 100 nM DHT. At D1 after keratinocyte seeding, cells were moved to an air–liquid interface to facilitate proper stratification of the skin equivalent. At D10, once the skin equivalent was properly stratified, a pulse of EdU was administered and chased for 72 h. No significant difference in cell proliferation was observed between control and DHT-treated cultures. However, following a 72-h chase, DHT-treated human keratinocytes presented an increased fraction of EdU-labelled suprabasal cells (37.2 ± 2.0% for DHT-treated and 24.6 ± 2.0% for control). This result shows that testosterone promotes delamination of human keratinocytes (Fig. 5h–j and Extended Data Fig. 12n).

Finally, to assess whether DHT promotes human keratinocyte differentiation, we performed in vitro culture of human-derived skin epithelioids46. In contrast to the 3DSE model, skin epithelioids allow keratinocytes to be cultured without a fibroblast layer (Extended Data Fig. 12o). Epithelioids presented a lower level of stratification than traditional skin equivalents, which precluded the quantification of stratification using EdU pulse–chase experiments. However, this technique enabled us to study the initial steps of differentiation. After 2 weeks of culture, epithelioids were starved in hormone-free medium for 72 h and subsequently treated for 72 h in the same medium with or without 100 nM DHT. Whole epithelioids, including basal and suprabasal layers, were collected and analysed by RT–PCR. Out of the three individuals (two male and one female), two samples presented an increase in differentiation (marked by upregulation of EVPL, IVL, KRTDAP, OVOL1 and TGM1) (Fig. 5k). Altogether, these results demonstrate that the male hormone DHT promotes epidermal differentiation and stratification in the absence of stroma or immune cells.

Here using long-term time-lapse imaging, we uncovered sex-specific differences in the clonal dynamics of epidermal SC and TA cells during adult homeostasis of the mouse ear skin. Our results showed that SC differentiation occurs through an intermediate TA-cell-like compartment together with a high degree of coordination between sister cells, both in the timing of cell division and in delamination. These findings are resonant with previous studies. In particular, clonal tracing based on the Krt10 promoter provided evidence for basal progenitors with limited proliferative potential15. Furthermore, continuous live-imaging of ear and paw skin epidermis showed evidence of temporal correlations in the delamination of sister cells4. Studies of the back skin epidermis under perturbed or regenerative conditions also showed evidence for an enhancement in even clone sizes, which indicated that there is the same proliferative heterogeneity in other epithelial contexts9. Here through long-term live-imaging, we showed how the enrichment of even clone sizes is integrated into individual lineage dependences and, in doing so, revealed substantial functional differences between male and female mice.

To develop a minimal model of SC–TA cell fate, we imposed a mechanism of SC density regulation based on mechanical feedback and correlated fate decisions with local cell density47,48. However, we note that mechanisms involving competition for niche factors49 or a combination of both biochemical and mechanical cues can lead to the same qualitative dynamics. Moreover, provided the timing of sister cell events remains temporally coordinated, we cannot rule out the potential of more complex models of cell fate involving, for example, further heterogeneity of the basal cell compartment or a low frequency of reversion between TA and SC identity.

Previous studies have shown the positive effect of oestrogen30,31 and the negative effect of androgens on skin wound-healing32. These phenomena have been attributed to the effect of sex hormones on the ECM and inflammatory response30. However, some reports have indicated that keratinocytes could be involved in this sexual dimorphism, as conditional deletion of the gene encoding oestrogen receptor-β (ESR2) in the epidermis phenocopies the delay in wound healing observed in mice in which Esr2 is completely knocked out50. Here we showed that wounds in female mice heal more rapidly than in male mice owing to more rapid basal cell activation in the initial stage of repair and a higher degree of cell coordination.

Alongside the development of reproductive systems, sex-specific differences have been reported in the number of cells that comprise tissues, neuronal connectivity and skin thickness both in animal models and in humans25,26,29. Sex-specific differences in SC functions have also been reported in several tissues such as the mouse haematopoietic system, the fly intestine and in the regenerative capacity of skeletal SCs51,52,53. In the fly gut, the difference is intrinsically regulated by a sex-specific transcriptional program52, whereas haematopoietic SCs are regulated by sex hormones, such as oestrogen produced by the ovaries53. In the context of mouse skin, where the epidermis is thicker in females whereas the dermis is thicker in males, the proliferation rate in the female epidermis is mediated by oestrogen received by the canonical oestrogen receptor (oestrogen receptor-α, encoded by Esr1)28. Whether this sex difference phenotype is the consequence of a different clonal dynamic of epidermal SC and TA cells is currently unknown. Here we observed a notable difference in the proliferative capacity of TA cells and the rate and coordination of basal cell delamination between male and female mice. This dynamic behaviour of TA cells depended on male hormones, as orchidectomy abolished the differences in clonal dynamics between male and female mice. Altogether, these data and previous studies indicate that there is sexual dimorphism in the homeostatic regulation of epidermal maintenance.

Differential gene expression between male and female mice uncovered sex dimorphic genes that were differentially regulated across all basal cell states, including X and Y chromosome genes, as well as sex dimorphic genes that were preferentially expressed in male or female mice in a particular cell state. Notably, this dimorphism was not limited to keratinization but extended to proliferation, ECM modification, actin network and inflammatory responses, with the inflammatory responses echoing the sex dimorphic presentation of highly prevalent inflammatory skin diseases such as atopic dermatitis54. Finally, most of the sex dimorphic differentiation genes were affected by male sex hormone deprivation after castration. Consistent with the delamination rate and clonal dynamic of castrated mice, molecular profiling in vivo and in vitro indicated the ability of male sex hormones to modulate the transcriptome and basal layer composition of IFE basal cells. Altogether, these results demonstrate the direct impact of male sex hormones on basal cell fate. However, we cannot rule out that immune cells or ECM components contribute to the observed sex differences, as they have been demonstrated to be sensitive to androgens29,55.

In conclusion, our long-term clonal analysis in vivo in living animals demonstrated the hierarchical organization of proliferative states in the IFE and uncovered a previously unknown hormonally regulated sexual dimorphism both in cell fate and temporal coordination of sister cells during homeostasis and wound healing. As epidermal SCs have a key role in skin cancer formation, it will be interesting to study the impact of sex-specific regulation of SCs during clonal expansion, field cancerization and tumour initiation.

Fat tissue and cartilage were removed, and skin was floated on a solution of 0.25% trypsin and EDTA 1 mM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in DMEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) overnight at 4 °C. The day after, the epidermis was carefully separated from the dermis by using forceps and then floated on a rocking plate (100 rpm) at 37 °C for 10 min on the trypsin mix. Tissue was cut into pieces, and epidermal cells were mechanically dissociated by pipetting with a 5 ml pipette. Trypsin was neutralized by adding DMEM supplemented with 5% Chelex-treated fetal calf serum (CFCS), and samples were filtrated through a 70-μm filter then through a 40-μm filter and washed in PBS supplemented with 2% CFCS (FACS buffer). Single cells were incubated with a biotinylated anti-CD34 primary antibody (clone RAM34, eBiosciences, 13-0341-82, 1:50) diluted in FACS buffer, incubated for 30 min on ice and protected from light. Cells were washed with FACS buffer then incubated for 30 min on ice with streptavidin–APC secondary antibody (BD Biosciences, 554067, 1:100) and CD49f (integrin-α6) PE-Cyanine7 conjugated antibody (clone eBioGoH3 (GoH3), Thermo Fisher Scientific, 25-0495-82, 1:250). The cells were washed again and resuspended in a Hoechst (10 mg ml–1) solution diluted at 1:4 000 in FACS buffer. Cell sorting was performed on a BD FACSAria III using FACSDiva software (BD Bioscience) at the ULB Flow Cytometry Platform. Live epidermal cells were gated by forward scatter, side scatter and negative for Hoechst. Bulge (hair follicle) cells were excluded by CD34 staining. Only basal cells (integrin-α6 positive) cells were sorted (Supplementary Fig. 7). A total of 100,000 cells were sorted for each sample (1 sample containing 2–3 different mice from the same litter).

Single-cell suspensions from two independent male mice and two independent female mice were loaded and multiplexed on a GEM-X OCM 3′ Chip kit (v.4 4-plex, PN-1000747, 10x Genomics) and barcoded with a Chromium X instrument according to the manufacturer’s recommendations (10x Genomics).

Libraries were prepared using a GEM-X Universal 3′ Kit (v4 4-plex, PN-1000779, 10x Genomics) and sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 (paired-end 100 bp reads).

Sequencing reads were aligned and annotated with the mm10-2020-A reference dataset as provided by 10x Genomics and demultiplexed using CellRanger (v.7.0.0)60 with default parameters.

Quality control and downstream analyses were performed using the R package Seurat (v.5.1.0)61. For each sample, cells were filtered using the following criteria: more than 2,000 and fewer than 6,000 unique genes; less than 7.5% UMI counts belonging to mitochondrial sequences; and less than 5% UMI counts belonging to ribosomal sequences. Doublets were detected using DoubletFinder (v.2.0.6)62 and scds (v.1.26.1)63 with default parameters. Cells with a doublet score on scds greater than or equal to 0.75 and labelled as doublet on DoubletFinder were filtered out.

To identify and retain IFE basal cells for downstream analyses, each sample was first processed and analysed independently. Read counts were normalized using the NormalizeData() function of Seurat, with the parameters ‘normalization.method = “LogNormalize” and scale.factor=10000’. A principal component analysis (PCA) for each sample was calculated using scaled expression data of the 2,000 most variable genes (identified as outliers on a mean/variability plot, implemented in the FindVariableFeatures() function). Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) calculation and graph-based clustering were performed for each sample using Seurat (default parameters) with the first 40 respective PCA components as input. The final clustering resolution for each sample was chosen to best reflect the underlying biological heterogeneity. On the basis of canonical marker gene expression, clusters corresponding to hair follicle SCs (Lgr5 and Lhx2) and infundibulum cells (Lrig1 and Sox9) were identified and excluded, whereas the remaining cells were retained as IFE basal cells for downstream analyses. Cell cycle phase scores (S phase and G2/M phase) were computed for each sample using the CellCycleScoring() function implemented in Seurat.

For comparison of transcriptomic profiles between male and female mice, the retained IFE basal cells from all samples were integrated using the standard CCA-MNN-based data integration workflow in Seurat. Feature selection was performed using the FindVariableFeatures() function from Seurat with default parameters, selecting the 2,000 most variable genes. Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) followed by integration anchor selection were then performed on the selected features using the FindIntegrationAnchors() function from Seurat, taking the first 40 dimensions from the CCA into account, as described in the standard workflow of Seurat. These anchors were then used to integrate data with the IntegrateData() function. This CCA-MNN workflow was used for two-dimensional visualization and cell-state annotation to maintain consistency with our previously established mouse IFE analysis framework16,22, which enabled direct comparison with previous studies while preserving the established annotation of G0/G1 A, G0/G1 B, G0/G1 C, S, G2/M, committed and differentiated cell states (Extended Data Fig. 7d). The assessment of gene expression was based on known marker genes from previous scRNA-seq data9,16,17,22 together with cluster-specific differentially expressed genes. In particular, the G0/G1-A cluster expressed stemness markers such as Lgr6, Trp73 or Smoc2, G0/G1-B was defined by S100a6 and high levels of Krt14, and G0/G1-C showed expression of Cdk8 or immune-related genes such as Il31ra. Committed and differentiated clusters expressed a gradient of differentiation genes with higher levels of Notch receptors (Notch1 and Notch2), Notch target genes (Hes1) and other TFs that regulate epidermal differentiation (Grhl1, Foxn1 and Gata3). Differentiated keratinocytes were characterized by upregulation of terminal differentiation makers, including Zfp750, Klf4, Irf6, Krt1, Krt10, Krtdap, Dmkn and Sbsn (Extended Data Fig. 7a–j).

Marker genes for each cluster were identified by Wilcoxon rank-sum test using the FindMarkerGenes() function in Seurat with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing. Only genes expressed in at least 25% of cells with an average log2 fold change of at least 0.35 and an adjusted P < 0.01 are reported. A small cell-dissociation-induced cluster marked by elevated levels of Jun, Fosb and Atf3 was subsequently removed from downstream analyses64 (Extended Data Fig. 7a).

To investigate the global organization of epidermal cell state transitions, we constructed a 3D diffusion manifold using the standard analysis workflow implemented in Pegasus (v.1.9.0)65. Highly variable genes were selected for per sample and used to calculate the first 50 principal components (PCs), which were batch-corrected using the run_harmony() function of Pegasus without cell cycle regression. Harmony was used in this context because it is natively integrated in the Pegasus workflow and provides efficient batch correction before construction of the diffusion map. The Harmony-corrected PCs were then used to compute 100 diffusion components via the diffusion map method (diffmap function of Pegasus). After excluding the first trivial diffusion component, the remaining components were embedded into three dimensions using the force-directed layout algorithm (fle function of Pegasus) to visualize the topology of the differentiation manifold while preserving cell cycle progression, which enabled interpretation of the relationships between cell identity, proliferation and lineage progression. The Python package scVelo (v.0.3.2)66 was used to run RNA velocity analysis, which was visualized on a force-directed layout manifold. The Python package dynamo (v.1.4.1)67 was used to estimate both the speed and acceleration of cellular transcriptomic dynamics.

To enable direct comparison with the three-state clonal modelling framework, Leiden clustering (resolution of 0.5), implemented in Scanpy (v.1.9.8)68, was applied to Harmony-corrected PCs after cell cycle regression, partitioning the data into three major biological populations. This approach ensured that clustering is driven by transcriptional identity rather than cell cycle state or batch effects, which enabled the identification of biologically meaningful compartments independent of proliferation status. On the basis of canonical marker gene expression (for example, Trp73, Klf4 and Krt10) and transcriptomic profiles, these populations correspond to SC, TA and differentiated states, respectively. For downstream differential expression analyses, cells annotated as S and G2/M phases were retained but were assigned all cells to their corresponding biological populations (SC, TA and differentiated) based on the cell-cycle-regressed clustering, thereby minimizing confounding effects from cell-cycle-associated transcriptional programs.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defining individual cell populations (SC, TA and differentiated) were first identified at the single-cell level using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in the “rank_genes_groups” function in Scanpy. Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied to control the FDR, and genes expressed in at least 25% of cells (min.pct = 0.25), with a log2 fold change > 0.5 (logfc.threshold = 0.5) and an adjusted P < 0.01, were considered differentially expressed. To leverage biological replicates and to improve the robustness of marker identification, we also performed pseudobulk differential expression analysis using both edgeR and DESeq2 with the same significance criteria (log2 fold change > 0.5 and adjusted P < 0.01). Cell-type marker genes were defined on the basis of the consensus of these complementary analyses, thereby increasing the robustness of the identified marker genes. Given the substantially larger number of genes tested and the primary goal of defining robust cell-type markers, a more stringent significance threshold (adjusted P < 0.01) was applied.

DEGs between male and female mice were identified independently in each cell type (SC, TA and differentiated) using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in the “rank_genes_groups” function in Scanpy, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing. Given that sex-associated transcriptional differences were expected to be more subtle than differences between major cell populations, a less stringent adjusted P value threshold (0.05) was used to detect DEGs.

The R package SCENIC (v.1.3.1)69 was used to infer regulons—sets of TFs and their putative target genes—based on co-expression and DNA motif enrichment, following the standard SCENIC workflow. Regulon activity scores were computed at the single-cell level using the AUCell module implemented in SCENIC. Differential regulon activity was assessed using the R function t.test, comparing both across cell types and between male and female mice in each cell type, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Because differential regulon activity was evaluated across a substantially smaller number of features (221 inferred regulons) than gene-level differential expression analyses, an adjusted P < 0.05 was used to define significant differences in regulon activity.

FACS-isolated cells were collected into 350 µl lysis buffer (RLT buffer, RNeasy Microkit, Qiagen) supplemented with β-mercaptoethanol. RNA extraction was then carried out using a RNeasy Micro kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Before sequencing, the quality of RNA was evaluated by Fragment Analyzer (Agilent).

Indexed cDNA libraries were obtained using a NEBNext Single Cell/Low Input RNA Library Prep kit for Illumina (NEB E6420S) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The multiplexed libraries were loaded on flow cells and sequences were produced using a NovaSeq 6000 S2 Reagent kit (200 cycles from Novaseq 6000 System, Illumina) on a NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina).

The quality of each raw data was checked using FastQC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Adaptor sequences and low-quality regions were trimmed using TrimmomaticPE (v.0.39)70. Trimmed reads were mapped against the mouse reference genome (Grcm38/mm10) using STAR aligner (v.2.7.11b)71 to generate read alignments for each sample. Reference genome sequences and corresponding annotations for Grcm38.87 were obtained from ftp.Ensembl.org. Duplicate reads were removed using the Picard MarkDuplicates module (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). After transcript assembly, gene-level counts were obtained using HTseq (v.0.11.1)72 and normalized to 20 million aligned reads per count. The average expression of each gene for the different cell populations was computed based on at least two biological replicates, and fold changes were calculated between the subpopulations.

For DEGs across the conditions, genes with a fold change of expression ≥ 2 were considered as upregulated and those with a fold change of expression ≤ 0.5 were considered downregulated. Genes were excluded from the differential expression analysis if they had more than a threefold difference in expression between replicates. Gene expression of less than 10 normalized read counts in at least one individual were not considered for differential expression.

Differential expression analysis was then performed using the R package DESeq2 (v.1.46.0)73. Genes with very low expression were excluded by retaining only those with a total count greater than ten across all samples to reduce noise and to improve statistical power. Normalization was carried out using the median-of-ratios method implemented in DESeq2 to account for differences in sequencing depth. To stabilize variance across the dynamic range of expression values, regularized log (rlog) transformation was applied to the normalized counts. Quality assessment of the dataset was performed using multiple complementary approaches. Pairwise comparisons between biological replicates were examined to evaluate reproducibility, and sample relationships were further assessed using Euclidean distance-based clustering and correlation heatmaps. PCA was conducted on the transformed data to visualize global transcriptional differences between conditions, which confirmed appropriate clustering of samples according to experimental groups. Differential expression testing was performed using a negative binomial generalized linear model framework, as implemented in DESeq2. Dispersion parameters were estimated using empirical Bayes shrinkage, and significance was assessed using Wald tests. Pairwise contrasts were defined between male and female, male and castrated, and female and castrated conditions. Resulting P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, and genes with an adjusted P < 0.1 were considered differentially expressed. To improve the interpretability and stability of effect-size estimates, log2 fold changes were further shrunk using the normal shrinkage estimator.

In parallel, differential expression analysis was independently performed using the edgeR (v.4.4.2)74 package to validate the robustness of the results. A DGEList object was constructed from the raw count matrix, and lowly expressed genes were filtered by retaining those with counts per million greater than or equal to 1 in at least two samples. Library size normalization was performed using the trimmed mean of M-values method. A design matrix was constructed based on experimental groups, and gene-wise dispersions were estimated using empirical Bayes methods before fitting a generalized linear model. Differential expression testing in edgeR was conducted using likelihood ratio tests for the same pairwise contrasts defined in the DESeq2 analysis. Resulting P values were adjusted using the FDR method. Differentially expressed genes were ranked on the basis of significance and effect size and annotated with gene identifiers before export for downstream analyses.

To assess the consistency between differential expression results obtained from DESeq2 and edgeR, overlap analyses were performed using Venn diagrams. The significance of the overlap between gene sets was evaluated using a hypergeometric test, which estimates the probability of observing a given number of overlapping genes by chance, given the total number of genes tested. Specifically, for each pairwise comparison, the total number of genes included in the analysis was defined as the background population, and the sizes of gene sets identified by each method, as well as their observed intersection, were used to compute one-sided P values corresponding to enrichment of overlap. For a given comparison, the P value was calculated using the cumulative hypergeometric distribution as implemented in R (function phyper), testing the probability of observing at least k overlapping genes between the two methods.
