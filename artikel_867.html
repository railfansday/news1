Competing mechanisms toughen bcc binary and medium-entropy alloys at 20 K

Plastic deformation in body-centred cubic (bcc) metals and alloys is conventionally mediated by screw dislocations. Their temperature-sensitive motion is attributed to a steep rise in strength with decreasing temperature that constricts plastic zones at crack tips, leading to a ductile-to-brittle transition1. Here we evaluated the mechanical properties of group IV–V bcc refractory alloys with similar electronic structures at 20 K and found that these materials instead exhibit a plateau in tensile yield strength and fracture toughness as a function of temperature, with no apparent ductile-to-brittle transition. This behaviour arises from the global activation of twinning and an ω-phase transformation, which nucleate dislocations at interfaces before global dislocation motion. The initial mechanism is temperature insensitive and controls yielding, thereby preventing a crack-tip plastic zone from constricting with decreasing temperature.

Alloys with the body-centred cubic (bcc) crystal structure have a ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT) below which plastic deformation is restricted, leading to embrittlement1,2. This problem has plagued bcc materials ranging from steels at or slightly below room temperature to refractory alloys up to 1,200 °C (refs. 3,4). The expansive set of current and potential applications for bcc alloys, such as refractory multi-principal alloys (commonly known as medium- or high-entropy alloys), for building and transportation infrastructure, energy technologies and hypersonic applications, is often limited by this transition5,6.

This limitation arises from their non-closed-packed crystal structure, which forces dislocations, specifically screws, to rely on thermally activated kink-pair nucleation to move2,7. Consequently, screw dislocation glide becomes sluggish at low temperatures, causing strength to increase precipitously and plasticity to be constricted at crack tips until fully brittle failure ensues8. Traditional strategies to suppress the DBTT primarily rely on facilitating screw dislocation glide through careful substitutional and interstitial alloying9,10,11,12: although interstitial elements increase DBTTs by introducing additional pinning stresses, substitutional alloying can, in some cases, ductilize bcc materials11. This has been observed in refractory binary and multi-principal element alloys, which are often extremely brittle4,11,13. Here concentrated additions of group IV (Ti, Zr, Hf) into group V (V, Nb, Ta) elements enhance screw dislocation glide, a phenomenon correlated with lowered unstable stacking-fault energies and increased kink-pair equilibrium concentrations11,14.

Beyond promoting dislocation glide, additional strategies for improving toughness involve activating multiple deformation mechanisms15. Twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP) and transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) can elevate toughness by increasing strain hardening and uniform ductility15,16, while some softening mechanisms, such as kink banding, can also elevate toughness by promoting plasticity17,18. Although TWIP and TRIP mechanisms are highly effective at increasing cryogenic damage tolerance in face-centred cubic (fcc) alloys, their efficacy is limited in bcc alloys at low temperatures owing to fundamental differences in the mechanisms controlling nucleation, propagation and migration. This limitation arises from a competition between temperature-dependent dislocation glide stresses and more temperature-insensitive twinning stresses, which can converge at a slip-twinning transition19,20,21. Around this transition, it is hypothesized that the stress to transform 1/2<111> screw dislocation cores into three 1/6<111> fractional dislocations that bind a propagating twin embryo is lower than for the same dislocation to retain its compact core and glide22,23. This process can be accompanied by a phase transformation to the ω-phase, which can further decrease twin migration stresses and promote twin thickening24. Above this transition, twinning enhances ductility through the TWIP effect; however, near or below it, twins instead promote brittle failure by impinging on interfaces25,26,27. Even though twinning prevents further increases in yield stress (and therefore the constriction of the crack-tip plastic zone)25,27, the inherently high barrier to dislocation nucleation in alloys impedes the dissipation of damage at interfaces. For this reason, this transition causes brittle failure in tension (for example, in Fe–Si) and therefore is nearly always measured in compression, a stress state that closes cracks25,28.

Nevertheless, there is evidence that the slip-twinning transition can occur in alloys that do not exhibit a clear DBTT, such as Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 and an electronically similar, but chemically simpler, Nb70Ti30 alloy17,29. Previous work has demonstrated that Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 has exceptional strength and toughness across a wide temperature range owing to a combination of dislocation glide, twinning and kink-banding17. Together, these mechanisms sustain plasticity and suppress brittle failure, resulting in a fracture toughness of up to 90 MPa√m at 77 K. This combination of factors leaves the role of this transition in fracture behaviour in alloys that can retain tensile ductility at this mechanistic transition unclear. To understand the mechanical behaviour of these bcc alloys, we investigated their properties at even lower temperatures of approximately 20 K by performing uniaxial tensile tests and nonlinear elastic J-based fracture toughness tests, where the energy release rate, J, is measured as a function of crack extension, Δa. In addition to measuring bulk mechanical properties, we performed extensive post-fracture electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), fractography and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to investigate the plastic deformation mechanisms and their spatio-temporal evolution with increasing strain and stress. We found that these bcc alloys nucleate dislocations instead of cracks at twin- and ω-boundary intersection sites, preventing premature brittle fracture and enabling the plastic zone to form via temperature-insensitive mechanisms.

As described in more detail in Supplementary Information, the Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 and Nb70Ti30 alloys were arc-melted, forged and cross-rolled to a 33% reduction in thickness, and heat-treated at approximately 56% of their solidus temperature for 1 hour to produce a single-phase, near-fully recrystallized, equiaxed grain structure with an average grain size of approximately 259 μm for Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 and a heterogeneous, bimodal distribution of approximately 51–412 μm for Nb70Ti30 (Extended Data Fig. 1). Both alloys contain low interstitial concentrations: Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 has 0.059 at% (60 ppmw) C, 0.0495 at% (58 ppmw) N, and 0.146 at% (197 ppmw) O; Nb70Ti30 has 0.278 at% (561 ppmw) O and 0.0625 at% (111 ppmw) N (ref. 17).

Despite the high toughness and ductility at 20 K, the fracture surfaces in both alloys show differences between tensile and compact-tension specimens used for the fracture tests (Fig. 2a,b, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2, and Extended Data Fig. 3). Here it is important to note that in the vicinity of a crack, there are three principal stresses that are each approximately three to five times higher than the one principal stress in tensile specimens. The resultant triaxial stress state generates a hydrostatic stress, suppresses shear stresses and is additionally graded31. Whereas in uniaxial tension, fracture is fully ductile and occurs by microvoid coalescence, under crack-tip stress triaxiality, there are some signs of brittle features. Specifically, fracture is partly intergranular and partly ductile, with crystallographically oriented bands and microvoids along grain boundaries, a transition that departs from fully ductile fracture at higher temperatures. The differences generated between the two stress states are further illustrated by EBSD band-contrast and inverse-pole-figure (IPF) maps of tensile samples and at the mid-thickness of compact-tension samples, where plane-strain conditions and hydrostatic stresses are highest (Fig. 2c,d). Although both samples exhibit profuse {112}⟨111⟩ nano-twinning, tensile samples have higher levels of dislocation glide than compact-tension samples. In the latter, there are some kink bands (significantly fewer than at higher temperatures), indicating edge dislocation glide.

To address how this global sequence of tensile deformation mechanisms (we use the word ‘global’ to exclude cases of microplasticity in which local regions may favour dislocation glide before twinning) affects the fracture properties, we investigated compact-tension samples at the mid-thickness (where the stress triaxiality would be highest owing to plane-strain conditions) by transmission Kikuchi diffraction (TKD) and 4D-STEM. Although the EBSD maps in Fig. 2 provide an overview of the crack-tip deformation mechanisms, it remains unclear why the fracture is nominally intergranular. To identify the cause, we performed TKD and 4D-STEM on regions near grain boundaries to capture the deformation mechanisms occurring in the regions of highest stress before crack extension. Focused-ion-beam lift-outs were taken from a grain boundary several micrometres ahead of a crack tip and from an intergranular fracture facet with the distinctive crystallographic deformation markings in Fig. 2a. Precise calculation of stress and strain using the Hutchinson–Rice–Rosengren (HRR) crack-tip plastic strain field is detailed in Methods. Reconstructed virtual dark-field images of 4D-STEM maps in Fig. 4a–c with indexed diffraction patterns in Supplementary Fig. 5 show similar deformation structures to those seen at yielding in tensile samples: fine nanotwins accompanied by an ω-phase impinging on a grain boundary. Additional instances of twins forming the crystallographic microvoids in Fig. 1e on fractured grain boundaries are documented in Extended Data Fig. 6. However, any intergranular fracture that it could cause is delayed by dislocations nucleated in the parent and neighbouring grain, some of which were identified as edge and mixed dislocations by a g ∙ b analysis, where g is the diffraction vector and b is the Burgers vector (Fig. 4d,e and Supplementary Fig. 6). The shear stresses are high enough for edge dislocations to glide and form microscale kink bands, as we observed with TKD in regions close to the fracture surface35 (Fig. 4f and Extended Data Fig. 6). Other dislocations form channels along which deformation is localized (Fig. 4a).

Our results indicate that nonlinear interactions among four deformation mechanisms—twinning, phase transformations, dislocation glide and dislocation nucleation—can lead to competitive behaviour among them as a function of temperature. This manifests below 77 K as a global sequence of tensile deformation mechanisms that nominally begins with twinning or a phase transformation and ends with dislocation glide. However, a pervasive question remains as to why the slip-twinning transition inhibits the DBTT in these NbTi alloys but causes low-temperature embrittlement in all other bcc materials observed so far27.

To discern this variable, we used experimental and computationally measured values to quantify each mechanism. First, we obtained twinning and dislocation glide stresses for both alloys. Twinning stresses were obtained from tensile curves, whereas screw (with the original compact core) and edge dislocation tensile glide stresses were calculated using, respectively, the Rao–Suzuki model and the Maresca–Curtin model at 20 K with an experimental strain rate of 1 × 10−3 s−1 (refs. 36,37,38). These values are listed in Table 1. Athough the tensile glide stresses are only about 30% higher in Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 compared with Nb70Ti30, these apparently small differences in uniaxial tension can be exacerbated at a crack tip by the high hydrostatic stresses3. This is easily visualized by mapping these stresses onto the HRR fields in Fig. 4g,h and Extended Data Fig. 7. As can be seen in Fig. 4g, the local shear stresses at the crack tip are high enough to activate twinning in Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 and Nb70Ti30, but they are not high enough in Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 to move pre-existing dislocations, except for within a small region close to the crack tip. Lower glide barriers in Nb70Ti30 generate a strain-controlled fracture, as evidenced by the high and homogeneous dislocation distributions (despite local damage produced at twin/ω-phase interface sites) (Extended Data Fig. 8) and increased ductile fracture surface features in compact-tension samples (Extended Data Fig. 3).

These observations illustrate that lowering glide stresses enables global activation of dislocation glide at a crack tip, but it is not necessarily the critical mechanism preventing brittle fracture because the toughness of Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 is still nearly 100 MPa√m. These experiments indicate that chemical complexity raises the glide stresses, which elevates high-temperature strength, albeit with a modest cost to toughness, but that it is not a variable involved in determining the slip-twinning transition or DBTT17. We deduce that it is the combination of low twinning migration stresses and, most critically, low barriers to heterogeneous dislocation nucleation that is primarily a function of the electronic structure. Our observation in two tensile stress states is that twin grain-boundary sites nucleate dislocations instead of cracks, which (1) enables the generation of a plastic zone by twinning, and (2) suggests that dislocation nucleation (not glide) is a primary driver of the DBTT. Although dislocation nucleation stresses are generally very high and often difficult to compute, density functional theory studies show that adding group IV elements reduces the unstable stacking-fault energy of group V metals and alloys, a proxy for dislocation nucleation barriers11. These elements concomitantly promote twinning and the ω-phase transformation23,39. However, by promoting the transformation to the ω-phase, a phase predicted by the Rice parameter to be brittle, the group IV elements become a ‘double-edged sword40’. This transformation is especially problematic for samples containing cracks because high hydrostatic stress promotes stress-controlled transformations and exploits the new phase’s brittleness to accelerate fracture41,42. Density functional theory calculations at 0 K reveal that in NbTaTiHf alloys, increasing group IV concentrations promote lattice instabilities from bcc to ω-phase (ref. 39). At 30 at% Ti and Hf, the bcc phase in Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 is still relatively stable39. But the composition is heterogeneous. Atom probe tomography analysis of grain boundaries in Extended Data Fig. 9a,b shows that Ti and Hf enrich the boundaries in an approximately 3 nm range, raising the group IV concentration to 42 at% in the measured region. A TEM energy-dispersive X-ray analysis (Extended Data Fig. 9c–h) of the grain boundary from Fig. 4 at the twin/ω-phase impact site indicates that this boundary is also enriched in Ti and Hf. This produces a local environment that promotes dislocation nucleation, but it also produces an additional chemical driving force for stress-induced ω-phase formation43.

Our findings demonstrate that NbTi-based alloys can retain exceptional damage tolerance, with no measurable DBTT observed down to 20 K. These properties are derived from a global mechanistic sequence that is unusual for bcc alloys in tension, which are often susceptible to high-temperature DBTTs4. Whereas most metallic alloys (particularly those with the fcc structure) generate high toughness from a global sequence that begins with dislocation glide and progresses to twinning and a phase transformation that delocalizes dislocation-induced damage15,17, the global sequence in the current alloys begins with twinning (mediated by screw dislocation core transformation) coupled to a phase transformation that nucleates dislocations to dissipate stress concentrations produced by the former two mechanisms. Dislocation glide subsequently follows. This achieves an unconventional pathway to inhibit a DBTT: crack-tip blunting is initiated by twinning, a near-temperature-insensitive mechanism that prevents a crack-tip plastic zone from shrinking and serves as an efficient dislocation source. Our results illustrate another pathway, independent of chemical complexity, for inhibiting the DBTT in refractory bcc alloys.

The Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 alloy was initially designed to balance room-temperature ductility and high-temperature strength, according to ref. 17. In summary, the original rationale for the chosen composition was to introduce a high concentration of group IV elements to promote ductility, but not too much as to promote the formation of brittle hexagonal close-packed and ω-phases. We did not include Zr because of its tendency to act as a secondary phase former44. The Nb/Ta ratio was chosen to promote lattice distortion while simultaneously minimizing the concentration of Ta because of its high density.

Although these studies were useful for the high-temperature applications of these alloys, they gave little indication of the low-temperature behaviour. Density functional theory and molecular dynamics simulations using a machine-learning atomic potential for this alloy indicated that the screw dislocation glide stresses had well exceeded the measured yield strength at 77 K (ref. 38). Moreover, our experimental observations at 77 K suggested that the slip-twinning transition had already occurred and that the yield strength was controlled by twinning rather than dislocation glide17. The combination of experimental observations and simulations motivated the evaluation of the mechanical behaviour of this alloy at even lower temperatures to assess the role of this mechanistic transition on the DBTT.

The composition of the Nb70Ti30 alloy was chosen to maintain the valence-electron count of Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 while reducing the chemical complexity to determine whether this variable is important for suppressing the DBTT. Tensile yield strength values in ref. 31 suggested that this transition could possibly occur in this alloy as well. To further motivate investigating this material, we calculated screw dislocation glide stresses considering a kink width of ten Burgers vectors and the same methodology and interaction energies given in ref. 40. We found that the screw glide stresses were slightly lower than those for the multi-principal element alloy: 930 MPa and 1,080 MPa at 77 K and 20 K, respectively. The edge dislocation glide stresses calculated using the Maresca–Curtin model at 77 K and 20 K are 75 MPa and 105 MPa, respectively. This provided an opportunity to assess the role of chemical complexity on the DBTT and indirectly evaluate whether the operation of Frank–Reed sources controls the DBTT45. Our results suggest that electronic structure is the primary variable controlling this mechanistic transition and the DBTT, and that the relative velocity of screw and edge dislocations does not inhibit dislocation multiplication and glide. This latter point can also be rationalized by the rarity of Frank–Reed sources in single-phase materials.

The absence of notable strain hardening in tension is also of particular interest, as this property is often associated with high toughness in TWIP and TRIP alloys, exemplified by CrCoNi (ref. 15). In these fcc systems, twinning and a phase transformation follow dislocation glide to elevate strain hardening and, in turn, ductility and toughness46,47. Conversely, in the bcc systems, activation of twinning before dislocation glide arises from an intrinsic competition between dislocation motion and dissociation into a twin embryo22. This process instantly transforms grains into ‘multi-lane highways’ of varying Schmidt factor (Extended Data Fig. 5b,c). As the applied stress increases, glide is progressively favoured over twinning, with approximately 50% of the grains in the anti-twinning orientation expected to deform by screw dislocation glide with the original compact core. Dislocations with the lowest slip barriers are selected—specifically those that can cross-slip along twins—channelling them along these boundaries. The ω-phase, as a twin transitional structure, can further promote channelling by preventing dislocation transmission across twin boundaries. In regions where the ω-phase spans the distance between adjacent twins (Fig. 3b), dislocations can accumulate, similar to materials with pre-existing ω-phase particles48. Although this hampers uniform ductility, it assists sustained necking, leaving the total fracture strain high and nominally elevating toughness17.

Here we address three important microstructural variables that can impact the DBTT, and the resulting limitations this imposes on this study. First, the DBTT is strongly dependent on the concentration of interstitials in all bcc alloys. Studies on group IV–V multi-principal element alloys suggest that oxygen can have a particularly pronounced impact on strength and ductility9. Although multiple experiments illustrate that the addition of this interstitial increases room-temperature ductility by promoting strain hardening, it can simultaneously raise the DBTT to above 77 K (refs. 9,49). It is also known to raise twinning and phase transformation stresses, thereby suppressing these mechanisms21,40. However, this interplay is highly nonlinear, that is, O interstitials suppress the ω-phase transformation more relative to twinning21. Further work to understand the role in suppressing the ω-phase on twin embryo growth and propagation is needed. The relatively low interstitial concentration in Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 and Nb70Ti30 suppresses additional pinning stresses that would otherwise inhibit dislocation glide and twin migration. We postulate that additions of O to the material, probably above 1 at%, would raise the DBTT to above 20 K (refs. 9,50).

Grain size can also affect the DBTT of bcc alloys by raising both dislocation and twinning stresses through the Hall–Petch effect. Coarse grain sizes can be beneficial for increasing ductility and toughness by promoting dislocation glide. However, this only works up to a certain point. In fact, toughness and ductility begin to decrease with grain coarsening past a certain size owing to the promotion of strain localization51. Moreover, experimental work on multiple alloys indicates that materials undergo the slip-twinning transition across a large temperature range, and that increases in grain size can promote twin propagation, but potentially suppress twin boundary migration28,52. In Fe–Si, increasing grain size promotes embrittlement through this mechanism28. It is found that, like dislocations, twins obey a Hall–Petch relationship21. Further experimental work is needed to assess the Hall–Petch constants.

Finally, recrystallization has been shown to influence the DBTT. This effect is most pronounced in intrinsically brittle materials such as chromium and tungsten, in which extensive hot rolling can introduce high dislocation densities. The presence of these dislocations prevents the need to nucleate them at low temperatures, and owing to the inherent presence of kink pairs, these dislocations can more easily glide at low temperatures. As a result, materials such as tungsten and chromium can have their DBTTs suppressed by several hundred kelvin simply by introducing defects that are not entirely removed during recrystallization53,54. This effect also increases the cryogenic ductility of Nb and its alloys, but in refractory multi-principal element alloys it can substantially raise the yield stress owing to forest hardening, as is the case in Nb40Ta25Ti15Hf15Zr5 (refs. 55,56). When this alloy is not fully recrystallized, the room-temperature yield strength increases by nearly 100 MPa, reflecting limited dislocation glide that would compound at lower temperatures. This effect has been documented in another similar alloy57. As a result, partial recrystallization is beneficial by providing the material with already mobile dislocations, but it also introduces additional pinning sites for dislocations, thereby raising the strength and constricting plastic deformation.

The Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 was produced from high-purity elements (>99.9%), which were arc-melted and then flipped and remelted several times to ensure chemical homogeneity. The resulting 12-mm-thick ingots were forged and then rolled to approximately 8 mm (corresponding to a 33% reduction in thickness), and then heat-treated at 1,100 °C in air in sealed stainless-steel bags for 1 hour, followed by water quenching. This process was identical to that in ref. 17. The microstructure produced consisted of near-fully recrystallized grains approximately 259 μm in size.

The Nb70Ti30 was produced from high-purity elements (>99.9%) that were arc-melted, then flipped and remelted several times to ensure chemical homogeneity. The resulting 10-mm-thick ingots were then cross-rolled to approximately 6.7 mm (corresponding to a 33% reduction in thickness) and heat-treated at 1,077 °C in air in sealed stainless-steel bags for 1 hour, followed by water quenching. This heat-treatment temperature was chosen to be the same fraction of the solidus temperature (approximately 0.56) as Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15. The microstructure consisted of a bimodal grain distribution. Some grains were fully recrystallized and averaged approximately 51 μm, whereas others were partially recrystallized with an average diameter of approximately 412 μm. These grains were further subdivided by low-angle boundaries consisting of recrystallized rotational twins and kink bands.

To analyse the microstructure of the material, the samples were cut perpendicular to the rolling direction, embedded in resin and mechanically polished to a surface finish of 0.04 μm. The sample was then analysed by EBSD in a Thermo Fisher Scios 2 Dual Beam scanning electron microscope (SEM) at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. Bulk O and N interstitial concentrations were analysed by inert gas fusion and carbon by combustion.

To evaluate the tensile properties, three rectangular dog-bone specimens with a gauge length of 12 mm were electron-discharge machined from the ingots. All sides of the specimen were polished to 1000 grit SiC paper to a final cross-sectional area of 3.13 mm2. Tensile tests were performed under quasi-static conditions with a strain rate of 10−3 s−1. The strain was measured with a 10-mm 3442 clip-on extensometer (Epsilon Technology) rated to perform at temperatures as low as approximately 1 K.

The yield strength, σy, ultimate tensile strength, σu, uniform ductility, ε, and elongation to fracture, εf, were determined from the uniaxial engineering stress–strain curves. Corresponding true stress–strain (σT/εT) curves were calculated and then compared with instantaneous strain-hardening curves (dσ⁄dϵ) as a function of strain to evaluate the hardening behaviour. A strain-hardening exponent was additionally evaluated using the constitutive power-hardening law σT = /k(εT)n. Here n is the strain-hardening exponent and k is a scaling constant. However, the material did not exhibit ideal hardening behaviour, and consequently, n represents an average hardening value.

To evaluate the fracture properties, two compact-tension specimens were cut from the ingots by electron-discharge machining with nominal width W = 16 mm and thickness B = 3.5 mm. To prepare the samples for fracture by overload, an atomically sharp crack was inserted into the sample by fatigue pre-cracking. First, both faces were mechanically polished to a final finish of 0.04 μm to allow for crack-length measurement with a long-distance optical microscope. The machined notch, which was approximately 3.5 mm in length, was then micro-notched with a razor blade coated in a 6-μm diamond paste to a final notch root radius ρ ≈ 10 μm. Finally, samples were cyclically loaded in a 4.5-kN servo-hydraulic 810 MTS load frame operated using an Instron 8800 digital controller using load control under a constant-amplitude sinusoidal wave of load ratio R = 0.1 (where R is the ratio of the minimum to maximum load) at a frequency of 15 Hz. To ensure that the plastic zone around the pre-crack tip did not affect the fracture tests, the stress intensity was gradually reduced such that the stress intensity range, ΔK, was 10–12 MPa√m at a final pre-crack length in the range of a/W ≈ 0.6. Specimens were then side-grooved to a final thickness BN ≈ 3 mm to ensure the that crack followed the mode I loading path.

For the present study, the tensile and fracture toughness tests were performed at the ENGIN-X beamline at the ISIS Neutron and Muon Source at the Rutherford Appleton Laboratory at Harwell Campus, Oxfordshire, in the UK. The details of this set-up are described in ref. 15. In brief, it consists of an Instron 100-kN rated hydraulic load frame with an Instron 8800 Controller attached to a vacuum-sealed chamber. Two cryo-coolers are connected to the grips with a copper clamp and a series of laminated copper sheets to ensure optimal thermal conductivity. A set of grips and pins made from a high-strength and thermally conductive CuBe alloy were installed into the cold fingers. To further ensure that the temperature reached 20 K, thermally conductive copper grease was generally placed in all connective joints. The temperature was measured with two rhodium–-iron thermocouples attached to the grips directly adjacent to the samples. Owing to the small sample size, it was not possible to attach a thermocouple to the samples without direct interference with the strain field around the crack. To verify that samples reached 20 K, an additional, significantly larger stainless-steel compact-tension sample with a larger thermal mass (W = 18 mm, B = 9 mm) was placed in the grips with one of the thermocouples embedded in a copper foil attached to the backside of the sample. Temperatures in the grips reached 8–15 K, whereas those at the edge of sample reached 28–35 K. We approximate the crack-tip temperature as an average between the two: 18–25 K, which for simplicity we state as 20 K.

After testing, the broken samples were immersed in acetone to prevent oxidation. The remaining uncracked ligament in one compact-tension sample was fractured by fatigue; the other compact-tension sample was cut by electron-discharge machining at the mid-thickness (B/2). Fractographic analysis was performed on the fractured sample using a Thermo Fisher Scios 2 Dual Beam SEM at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV using secondary electrons. The sample sectioned at mid-thickness was polished to a final finish of 0.04 μm and imaged in the SEM at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV by EBSD with a step size range of 0.2–1.25 μm. Analysis of EBSD data was performed using Aztec Crystal, a commercially available software.

To understand the grain-boundary chemistry, two sharp needle-like specimens of (1) a fracturing grain boundary and (2) a grain boundary of a grain that did not undergo intergranular fracture were prepared by the focused-ion-beam lift-out and annular milling techniques using a FEI Helios 5 Hydra UX. Three-dimensional atom probe analysis was performed using a local electrode atom probe (CAMECA LEAP 6000 XR) in laser pulse mode with a pulse energy of 60 pJ at a temperature of 70 K.

STEM was carried out to characterize the defect structures in the vicinity of the crack. Site-specific TEM foil samples were cut from the fractured samples and thinned to electron transparency using focused-ion-beam milling (FEI, Helios G4 UX and FEI, Scios 2). Two TEM lamellas were trenched and lifted out from the fracture surface and across grain boundaries near the crack tip from compact-tension test specimens. TEM lamella preparation finished with a 5 kV followed by a 2 kV Ga+ polish. STEM, TEM, HRTEM and 4D-STEM imaging of samples were conducted on FEI TitanX and FEI ThemIS microscopes (operated at 300 kV) at the National Center for Electron Microscopy (NCEM, LBNL). We constructed a micromechanical model to predict the fracture toughness, KJIc, of bcc alloys that undergo a sequence of mechanisms that begins with twinning. Our model assumes that dislocation glide is only activated by twinning and that the screw glide stresses are significantly higher than the twinning stresses, such that plasticity is solely controlled by twinning and subsequent edge glide. We postulate that semi-brittle intergranular cleavage occurs when the local stress calculated by the HRR field exceeds the upper-bound twinning stress. We first consider the largest difference between the principal stress calculated from the HRR field directly in front of the crack tip, that is, when θ = 0°, to consider effects from constraint.

On the basis of our postulate, fracture occurs when Δσmax exceeds the twinning stress at a critical microstructural length scale. Given that the upper-bound twinning stress is the yield strength, fracture occurs when Δσmax = σy at r = mdg, where m is an integer multiple of the grain-boundary diameter, dg.

The DBTT can be assessed with multiple measurements. Typically, the mechanical properties of a material at the DBTT are characterized by an increase in strength, cessation in tensile ductility and/or a decrease in fracture toughness. It can also be measured by an examination of the fracture mode with the change from ductile fracture (for example, microvoid coalescence) to the onset of transgranular cleavage and/or intergranular fracture, thus marking the transition. Although the NbTi alloys show a reduction in tensile ductility and toughness from room temperature, and a transition to a nominally intergranular fracture, the materials still have toughness of between approximately 100 MPa√m and 170 MPa√m, and tensile fracture strains of approximately 14%. The presence of significant plasticity ahead of the crack tip additionally indicates that the material does not exhibit brittle fracture behaviour and thus shows no DBTT.

The effect of stress-state is illustrated by the differences in fracture morphology observed between C(T) and uniaxial tensile samples. Whereas in C(T) specimens in (a) fracture occurs by crystallographic microvoids on grain boundaries, in tensile specimens in (b) it occurs by microvoid coalescence. This is further exemplified by IPF and BC EBSD maps that show profuse {112} < 111> twinning in (c) compact-tension and (d) tensile specimens, but with significantly more deformation by dislocation glide in tension in (d). The intergranular fracture can also be noted in (c), where the crack travels along the high-angle grain boundary between two large grains.

(a) Shows twinning at the onset of yielding, which progressively gives way to deformation by dislocation glide in (d). (b)-(c) A simple Schmidt factor analysis of the microstructure shows that the twins can orient each lamella into directions with varying critical resolved shear stress for both a0/2{110} < 111> and a0/2{112} < 111> slip systems. While in a typical TWIP material, this would generate strain hardening, here dislocation glide is difficult and plastic deformation has already begun, so only dislocations with the lowest barriers to glide (those with the highest critical resolved shear stresses) will move, causing strain to localize in the material. Severe deformation in the region that necked and directly adjacent to the fracture surface can be seen in (e). Note that the kink band-like features in (d) are part of the original microstructure.

(a) Overview EBSD map of crack-tip deformation structure with kink bands boxed in (b), (c), and (d). Pole figure maps of the kink bands in the selected areas in (a) (that contain kink bands) show that all have <110> poles, indicating that they are formed by a0/2 < 111 > {112} edge dislocations. The bands have variable rotation: those in (b) 8–15°, those in (c) 5–17°, and those in (d) 10–22°. These kink bands can also have gradient boundaries, indicating imperfect edge dislocation alignment, and can be micrometer-scale in size, as shown in (b). (e)-(f) show TKD micrographs of twins impinging on fractured grain boundaries at the fracture surface. The twin in (e) has an unusual morphology characterized by branching.

(a) A plot of the HRR field at 20 K as a function of distance from the crack tip normalized by the crack-tip opening displacement, δ. These values are compared with twinning growth stresses and screw and edge glide stresses. As can be seen, the applied stress at the critical stress intensity exceeds the twinning stress across a critical microstructural length-scale - a single grain size. The same occurs at 77 K in (b), demonstrating that the crack-initiation toughness is insensitive to temperature. However, at 77 K, screw glide stresses are lower, which causes this mechanism to activate during crack growth when the stress intensity increases. (c)-(d) show the local equivalent plastic strain as a function of distance from the crack tip. In Nb70Ti30, fracture is strain-controlled because both twinning stresses and dislocation glide stresses are lower than the fracture stress across a critical microstructural length-scale.
