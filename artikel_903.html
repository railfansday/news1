Neuronal microexons are a highly conserved class of alternative splicing events enriched in genes with critical functions in nervous system biology1,2. Microexon splicing is often disrupted in the brains of autistic individuals, and mice with microexon deficiencies display autism-like phenotypes1,3,4. However, the full landscape of microexons is not known, and the impact of genetic variation on these exons has not been systematically investigated. Here, through extensive transcriptome profiling, we detect hundreds of new microexons, including many with neuronal subtype-specific splicing patterns, and others that activate nonsense-mediated mRNA decay. Using machine learning, we define a sequence code predictive of neuronally spliced microexons and use this to identify critical regulatory elements surrounding the expanded microexon repertoire. This code further reveals genomic variants predicted to alter microexon splicing in individuals affected by autism, with rare variants having the strongest estimated impact. These variants are mostly detected in idiopathic individuals and are associated with autism status. They also converge on synaptic and other autism-linked genes, as well as genes not previously linked to autism. Our results collectively define an expanded repertoire of microexons, delineate their regulatory code, and pinpoint variants predicted to affect their splicing, with implications for the disruption of functions central to neuronal biology and brain disorders.

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a highly heterogeneous condition. Common characteristics of affected individuals include altered social and communication abilities, and repetitive behaviours. Hundreds of genetic alterations have been linked to ASD5,6, and approximately 130 genes have been confidently associated with ASD susceptibility, with functions that converge on transcriptional and chromatin regulation, synaptic signalling and translational control7,8,9. However, high-confidence ASD-associated variants are detected in only around 15% of affected individuals. Among the approximately 85% of idiopathic individuals, missing genetic aetiology may be attributable, in part, to variants overlapping non-coding genomic regions with important yet difficult-to-predict functions10.

Alternative splicing, a critical process by which exon and intron sequences are differentially included in transcripts to generate functional and regulatory diversity, is widespread in the mammalian nervous system11,12. Neuronally enriched, 3–27-nucleotide-long microexons represent a highly conserved class of alternative splicing1,2. Most annotated microexons preserve reading frames, and characterized examples function in modulating protein–protein interactions required for nervous system development and function3,4. Consistently, mice haploinsufficient of the major activator of neuronal microexons, Srrm4, display autistic-like neuroanatomical, electrophysiological and behavioural features13. Moreover, perturbations of individual microexons also display ASD-related transcriptomic, neurodevelopmental and behavioural phenotypes3,4,14,15,16,17.

Transcriptome-wide profiling of post-mortem brain samples from ASD-affected individuals has revealed recurring, distinct patterns of altered gene and splice isoform changes18,19,20, with the most prominent splicing dysregulation affecting microexons1. This dysregulation may arise in part through factors that affect neuronal activity, as the altered transcriptomic patterns correlate with those detected in activity-stimulated neural cells13,19. However, whether and to what extent microexon splicing is directly affected by human genetic variation linked to neurodevelopmental disorders is not understood. Moreover, the extent to which microexons remain undetected is unclear, as previous studies focused primarily on analysing RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data from intact brain tissues, potentially missing the identification of microexons spliced in specialized neuronal subtypes or those that trigger transcript instability.

Here we address these questions through an integrative analysis encompassing the identification of an expanded repertoire of neuronal microexons, as well as the development of a machine learning model that delineates sequence elements critical for microexon splicing, which can be used to predict the impact of genetic variants on microexon regulation. Through these approaches, we reveal predominantly rare human genetic variation predicted to convergently affect a subset of microexons in genes with known and unknown genetic and functional links to nervous system biology and neurodevelopmental disorders.

To define a more comprehensive set of microexons, we applied a de novo detection method21 using a precomputed set of cDNA sequences representing the inclusion and skipping of all putative microexons, of which there are approximately 2 million across human introns (Fig. 1a). Putative microexons are defined here as intronic regions of 3–27 nucleotides flanked by sequences resembling splice sites, but which were not previously detected in spliced transcripts. From analysing 4,790 whole tissue RNA-seq samples from the human brain and other sources, and data from 47,380 single nuclei from human cortical regions22, we detect 1,514 microexons not previously annotated in GENCODE or VastDB (Methods), of which 292 display differential splicing between neural and non-neural cells and tissues. Approximately 43% of these are detected in both bulk and single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) datasets (Methods, Extended Data Fig. 1a–d and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Similarly, for mouse, by analysing RNA-seq data from 297 whole tissue samples, 133 targeted translating ribosome affinity purification sequencing (TRAP-seq) datasets and two single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) profiling datasets, the latter comprising 25,479 cells derived from primary visual and anterior lateral motor cortex23,24, 1,115 new microexons were identified, of which 95 are detected as neurally spliced, often in multiple datasets (Extended Data Fig. 1e and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Compared with putative microexons, the newly detected microexons are associated with higher 5′ and 3′ splice site strength scores and higher levels of surrounding sequence conservation (Fig. 1b–d; P < 3 × 10−3, two-sided Mann–Whitney U-tests), and are in genes expressed, on average, at lower steady-state levels compared with those containing previously annotated microexons, but more highly expressed than genes with putative microexons (Fig. 1e; P < 2 × 10−4, two-sided Mann–Whitney U-test with Bonferroni correction). Consistent with these observations, compared with annotated microexons, the new microexons more often introduce premature termination codons (PTCs) predicted to induce nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD), and those that preserve reading frame less often overlap protein domains and intrinsically disordered regions (Fig. 1f and Extended Data Fig. 1f; P = 1.6 × 10−14, two-sided χ2 test with Yates’ correction).

Hierarchical clustering of the new microexons across the analysed samples on the basis of percentage spliced-in (PSI) measurements reveals that they generally have higher inclusion levels in snRNA-seq compared to bulk tissue RNA-seq data (Fig. 1g, Methods and Extended Data Fig. 1b,c,g–i), including those with NMD-inducing potential, consistent with NMD requiring cytoplasmic translation (Fig. 1g). Supporting a regulatory role, genes containing predicted NMD-inducing known and new microexons, relative to frame-preserving microexons, have significantly reduced mRNA expression in brain RNA-seq data (Fig. 1h; P = 0.0068, two-sided Mann–Whitney U-test). Moreover, cultured mouse glutamatergic neurons treated with cycloheximide (which inhibits NMD by blocking translation) display increased splicing of candidate NMD-inducing microexons, on the basis of RNA-seq analysis, an effect that is strongly enriched among newly identified microexons, and assays using PCR with reverse transcription (RT–PCR) validated increased levels for 8 out of 11 tested examples (Fig. 1i,j, and Extended Data Fig. 2a). Similarly, treatment of cultured human neurons with the NMD inhibitor SMG1i resulted in increased detection of 4 out of 7 analysed new, predicted NMD-inducing microexons (Extended Data Fig. 2b). Consistent with the larger proportions of new human and mouse microexons (relative to known microexons) predicted to elicit NMD, and evidence that NMD-inducing alternative exons often are less conserved than regulated coding exons25, a relatively small percentage (12.0%) of the new neural-differential human microexons have clear 1:1 orthologues in mouse. Collectively, these results reveal numerous examples of new microexons with neural-differential regulation and potential to control gene expression through activation of NMD, thus broadening the functional scope of this class of alternative splicing events.

A subset of the new microexons analysed in mouse display marked neuronal subtype-specific splicing (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 3a). These include examples in genes with functions in cell migration and embryonic brain development (Actn4), vesicle fusion and synaptic transmission (Scfd2) and transcriptional control of neuronal development and survival (Klf7) (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 3a–d). A subset of new microexons displays dynamic splicing changes during embryonic and postnatal mouse brain development, including examples in genes such as Ralgapa1, Gtf2i and Psd2 that are crucial for neurodevelopment and implicated in brain disorders (Fig. 2b–d).

To assess whether developmentally regulated microexons predicted to trigger NMD affect gene expression, we systematically compared correlations between changes in microexon PSI and levels of the corresponding transcripts, across mouse embryonic stages and brain tissues (Methods). Consistent with NMD limiting detection of changes in transcript levels containing PTC-introducing microexons, increased detection of NMD-inducing microexons is, on average, modestly yet significantly correlated with decreased transcript levels, as compared to frame-preserving microexons with comparable developmental changes in PSI (mean ρ = −0.10 versus +0.11; P = 0.0046, two-sided Mann–Whitney U-test; Extended Data Fig. 4a). Overall, we identified 34 mouse microexons predicted to elicit NMD that are regulated during brain development, including 7 newly identified in this study (examples are shown in Extended Data Fig. 4b,c and Supplementary Table 3).

The expanded landscapes of microexons detected in human and mouse are significantly enriched in genes associated with Gene Ontology (GO) terms representing critical nervous-system-specific processes (Fig. 2e (data for mouse) and Extended Data Fig. 5 (data for human)). The newly identified microexons are additionally enriched in terms related to the Rho family of small GTPases, focal adhesion and lamellipodia (Fig. 2e, Extended Data Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 4). Collectively, these results reveal more extensive repertoires of microexons with neuronal subtype and developmental splicing patterns, as well as NMD-activating potential, than previously appreciated. These repertoires serve as a basis for investigating the impact of genetic variation on microexon regulation and function.

To define a sequence code that controls neuronal microexon splicing, we developed a machine learning approach that discriminates sequences surrounding true-positive microexons (detected by RNA-seq) from those surrounding a background, negative set of microexon-resembling sequences (not detected by RNA-seq) (Fig. 3a). For training, we compiled a positive dataset comprising around 1,800 neural-differential microexons from human, mouse and additional mammalian species, and a negative dataset consisting of 91,920 intronic sequences matching known microexons in terms of length, flanking splice site strength score distributions, and additional contextual features, such as their locations within genes and surrounding intronic GC content (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 6a). As the number of true-positive microexons was insufficient to train effective deep neural networks, we adopted a transfer-learning approach. Specifically, we trained gradient boosted decision trees that incorporate, as input features, the outputs of previously described deep-learning models of general splicing regulation and RNA-binding protein (RBP) binding26,27,28. Additional features used by the code comprise characteristics of 5′ and 3′ splice sites, branch sites, nucleotide composition biases, and experimentally derived binding profiles and motifs of diverse RBPs (Extended Data Fig. 6b,c and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). Cis-regulatory elements that control tissue-dependent alternative exons are typically concentrated within sequences surrounding the exons, but also overlapping the immediate upstream and downstream exons and their flanking intron sequences29,30. Accordingly, the model learns features within these regions to discriminate the positive from the background set of microexon-associated sequences (Fig. 3a). The output of the resulting ‘microexon code-model’ is a score that estimates the likelihood that an input sequence represents a neuronally spliced microexon, on the basis of the composition of cis-RNA features overlapping these regions.

A train–test routine distinguished the positive set with a classification performance of 0.72 area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) (Extended Data Fig. 6d), which is a 45-fold increase in AUPRC over the baseline expected from random classification (AUPRC = 0.016). This performance was not significantly affected by excluding splicing-related features or orthologous sequences from test data that are present in training datasets (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 6e). Further supporting its performance, the model assigns significantly higher scores to known microexons analysed in Fig. 1 that have neuronal splicing patterns, compared to microexons that are constitutive, or spliced in subsets of non-neural samples (Extended Data Fig. 6f; P ≤ 0.001, Mann–Whitney U-test with Bonferroni correction). Moreover, although the model was not trained to predict PSI levels, its scores correlate more highly with PSI measurements for microexons in neural versus non-neural tissues (Extended Data Fig. 6g; Mann–Whitney U-test, P < 10−9).

To assess the accuracy of our model in pinpointing critical regulatory elements, we quantified the impact on prediction scores of in silico saturation mutagenesis (ISM) of each microexon and its neighbouring sequences described above (Fig. 3a,b and Extended Data Fig. 7a). Consistent with previous results indicating that microexons are on average associated with relatively strong 5′ splice sites and suboptimal 3′ splice sites1,31,32, in silico mutation of 5′ splice site sequences has the largest impact on prediction scores, followed by mutations at 3′ splice sites (Fig. 3b). Moreover, the average predicted impact of mutations across the surrounding intronic regions strongly correlates with vertebrate genomic conservation of the corresponding sequences (Fig. 3b and Extended Data Fig. 7b; Pearson’s r = 0.92, P < 1 × 10−100). For example, intronic sequences between 0 and −90 are predicted to have greater regulatory importance, consistent with this region encompassing higher evolutionary conservation, as well as concentrating cis-motifs critical for microexon splicing1,32 (Fig. 3b,c; see below). Applying the model to the new neuronal microexons reveals that 59 of these have ISM profiles resembling those of known microexons (Extended Data Fig. 7c–f). This subset may therefore be spliced using similar rules as known neuronal microexons, whereas other new microexons may be regulated by rules not captured by our model.

To further assess performance of the microexon code-model, we compared PSI changes observed after experimental mutagenesis of splicing reporters with changes in code scores resulting from corresponding in silico mutations. PSI changes resulting from mutagenesis of motifs corresponding to putative intronic SRRM4- and PTBP1-binding sites proximal to microexons correlate with code score changes (Spearman’s ρ = 0.62, P = 1.36 × 10−6, 10 microexons and 49 mutations analysed; Extended Data Fig. 8a and Supplementary Table 7). Furthermore, using data from massively parallel human microexon reporter splicing assays performed in HEK293 cells engineered to inducibly express SRRM4 (to mimic a neuronal-like context)33, we observed significant correlations between changes in PSI measurements and in silico code scores at three levels of SRRM4 expression (Spearman’s ρ = 0.049–0.115, all P < 4 × 10–10, 128 microexons and 16,258 analysed mutations; Supplementary Table 8).

To assess how changes in code scores compare to changes in PSI in response to variants in vivo, we analysed variants within 300-nucleotide windows surrounding microexons detected in whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data, using patient-matched RNA-seq data from PsychENCODE brain samples20,34,35. The top and bottom 5th percentiles of variant-dependent code score changes correlate significantly with changes in PSI measurements (Extended Data Fig. 8b; Pearson’s r = 0.25, P = 2.6 × 10−4). Moreover, our code-model compares favourably in predicting variant-dependent PSI changes from the reporter and in vivo brain datasets to Pangolin-Brain36 (Pearson’s r = 0.24, P = 4.8 × 10−4) and DeltaSplice37 (Pearson’s r = 0.23, P = 1.3 × 10−3), two previously described convolutional neural network (CNN) models trained on brain RNA-seq data and designed to predict PSI measurements (Methods, Extended Data Fig. 8b,c and Supplementary Table 9). Illustrating specificity, predictions from Pangolin models trained on heart, liver and testis RNA-seq data do not significantly correlate with variant-dependent ΔPSI measurements in patient brain RNA-seq data (Extended Data Fig. 8b). However, the Pangolin-Brain and DeltaSplice correlations are largely driven by a single (+1) 5′-splice-site-disrupting variant associated with the NRG1 microexon; removing this datapoint abolishes significance for all but the microexon code-model (Pearson’s r = 0.17, P = 0.034). This suggests that the microexon code-model is effective at capturing regulatory features relatively distal to core splice site dinucleotides, pinpointing cis-regulatory elements that are critical for microexon splicing and predicting effects of variants surrounding these exons.

To characterize the regulatory code learned by our model, we measured the impact of in silico mutagenesis of all hexamer sequences overlapping all known and the 59 new human microexons with similar ISM profiles. Mutations with a relatively large impact are enriched in subsets of related hexamer sequences resembling binding sites of known RBPs important for microexon regulation, including SRRM4, SRSF11, PTBP1 and RBFOX1,2,32 (Fig. 3c). The code further correctly maps the location of these motifs. For example, UGC and C/U repeat-containing motifs upstream of the 3′ splice site represent experimentally defined bipartite microexon splicing enhancer sequences bound by SRRM4 and SRSF11, respectively32 (Fig. 3d).

Binding sites for undetermined or less-well-established regulators are also predicted by the code. For example, a prominent role for CUAAY motifs, primarily residing between −20 to −40, is inferred (Fig. 3c). While this motif corresponds to one of multiple possible branch site sequences38, it also matches recognition sites of Quaking (QKI), a multifunctional RBP that controls alternative splicing, and that is linked to brain disorders39. To investigate a role for QKI in microexon regulation, we depleted it in mouse neuroblastoma (N2A) cells using small interfering RNAs (siRNAs; Extended Data Fig. 9a,b) and performed RNA-seq. Microexons proximal to consensus QKI-recognition motifs40,41,42 (that is, ACUAAY) within upstream introns show increased inclusion after Qki knockdown compared with those without QKI motifs40,41,42 (P < 0.01, Wilcoxon test; Fig. 3e and Extended Data Fig. 9c). Moreover, these motifs peak within −20 to −40 nucleotides upstream of QKI-repressed microexons, consistent with the in silico predictions, including those from a targeted ISM analysis of QKI-binding motifs (Extended Data Fig. 9d,e). To link the ACUAAY motif to QKI regulation, we generated minigene splicing reporters containing the QKI-repressed mouse microexons Ppfia1 and Abi2, which contain upstream intronic QKI consensus binding motifs (Fig. 3f). Knockdown of Qki reveals that microexons from the reporters with the wild-type (WT) motif (ACUAAC) are upregulated after Qki knockdown, whereas mutation of the motif to ACUGAC (bold indicates the mutation), which preserves predicted overlapping branchpoints but disrupts the QKI-binding site, abrogates this effect (Fig. 3f). These results further support the efficacy of the code in inferring known and undefined regulatory elements surrounding microexons.

Variants with the largest sibling-pair Δcode scores were classified by their allele frequency (AF) across reference populations44 as common (AF ≥ 1 × 10−2), rare (AF < 1 × 10−2, ≥1 × 10−5) or ultrarare (AF < 1 × 10−5) and individually analysed. Within the analysed genomic regions, 43% contain a single dominant variant, and 57% are associated with two or more predicted impactful variants (Extended Data Fig. 10a–c). Notably, there is a marked inverse relationship between the frequency of these variants, and the magnitude of their predicted impact on sibling-pair Δcode scores (Extended Data Fig. 10d,e). Specifically, rare and ultrarare variants have a significantly greater predicted impact than common variants (Fig. 4c, P = 4.4 × 10−8, two-sided Mann–Whitney U-test). Consistently, DeltaSplice also yields a significant difference when comparing the predicted impact of (ultra)rare versus common variants on microexon PSI (Extended Data Fig. 10f). Variants overlapping highly conserved intronic regions exhibit more negative Δcode scores than those overlapping less-conserved intronic sequences (Fig. 4d; P = 1.6 × 10−8, two-sided Mann–Whitney U-test). When analysing combined annotation dependent depletion (CADD) scores—a genome-wide metric of variant deleteriousness that integrates various genomic features45—we observed that 22.7% of the microexon-associated variants in ASD-affected individuals have predicted pathogenic potential (Methods), with significantly higher sibling-pair Δcode scores relative to 77.3% of variants scored as lacking pathogenic potential (P = 0.0013, two-sided Mann–Whitney U-test). These results indicate that the microexon code-model predicts impactful variants, with concordant support from the independent variant effect predictors, DeltaSplice and CADD.

To further assess reproducibility and test whether a subset of microexons is recurrently affected by rare variants, we analysed WGS data from an independent cohort of 5,112 ASD-affected and 263 unaffected siblings from the MSSNG resource8. Within each cohort (SSC, SPARK and MSSNG), we focused on microexons with the largest (that is, top decile) Δcode scores between affected and unaffected individuals, that is, those linked to (ultra)rare variants. This analysis revealed a significant overlap between microexons predicted to be impacted by variants across the three cohorts (Fig. 4e; 216 out of 369, 58.5% in the three-way overlap; Bonferroni-adjusted empirical P = 4 × 10−7). When restricting to Δcode scores inferred from ultrarare variants only, we still observe a significant three-cohort overlap (Extended Data Fig. 10g; Bonferroni-adjusted empirical P = 4 × 10−7). These results reveal that a common subset of microexons is enriched in predicted code-disrupting (ultra)rare variants derived from independent cohorts of ASD-affected individuals.

We next evaluated differences in the predicted cumulative impact of microexon-disrupting variants detected in ASD-affected versus unaffected siblings, by computing for each sibling a cumulative score that represents the summation of code score differentials (with respect to the hg38 reference genome assembly), for all predicted high-impact variants associated with all (n = 369) analysed microexons (Methods). Using data from the aforementioned massively parallel microexon splicing reporter assay data, we defined a lower-bound variant-effect threshold corresponding to a mean change in PSI ≥ 5, thereby identifying a set of variants referred to below as predicted impactful (Extended Data Fig. 8d). ASD-affected siblings were observed to have significantly higher cumulative differential code scores, relative to their unaffected siblings, above this threshold (Fig. 4f; P < 0.001, one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test). Moreover, cumulative sibling-pair Δcode scores predict ASD status (P = 0.0083, sibling-pair-matched conditional logistic regression controlling for sex; Supplementary Table 10). Consistently, when performing the same cumulative-impact analysis using CNN-based models, DeltaSplice yields comparable results (Fig. 4f).

To further assess the association between predicted impactful microexon variants and ASD status, we performed a rare-variant generalized disequilibrium test (RV-GDT)46, which evaluates whether affected relatives carry a higher aggregate burden of specific variants compared to unaffected relatives, using phenotype permutations within pedigrees to obtain empirical P values. Using predictions from our code-model and DeltaSplice, predicted impactful microexon-disrupting variants were collected and analysed across 25,132 individuals, representing 7,391 families from SSC (n = 2,419), SPARK (n = 2,033) and MSSNG (n = 2,939), while stratifying variants by AF. Consistent with the cumulative-impact analysis, ultrarare variants prioritized by the code-model show a significant within-family association with ASD status (Fig. 4g,h; mean empirical P < 0.05 after Benjamini–Hochberg correction). Notably, while DeltaSplice predictions alone did not reach significance, an intersection set prioritized by both models did (Fig. 4g,h; mean empirical P < 0.05, after Benjamini–Hochberg correction). From these analyses, we defined a superset of 72 predicted impactful variants significantly associated with ASD, comprising 28 ultrarare variants prioritized by the code-model and 44 additional variants highlighted by both models (Fig. 4g,h). These variants are detected in 2.5% of ASD-affected individuals and absent from their unaffected siblings (Extended Data Fig. 10h). Finally, minigene reporter assays in human induced neurons show that representative rare and ultrarare variants included among the RV-GDT superset alter microexon splicing (Extended Data Fig. 10i and Supplementary Table 11). Notably, these examples include a variant that directly targets a microexon (but not its splice site consensus sequences) within the MAP4K4 gene. These results provide evidence that variants that are predicted to impact microexon splicing are significantly over-represented in ASD-affected individuals and associated with ASD status.

We next examined whether microexons associated with predicted impactful variants detected in ASD-affected individuals converge on specific processes and functions. Focusing on microexons predicted to be recurrently affected by rare variants (SSC/SPARK/MSSNG triple overlap) and associated with ASD status (RV-GDT superset), we observe convergence on genes that function in the formation and function of synapses (Fig. 5). These encode proteins that are localized to the presynaptic active zone, such as members of the PPFIA (liprin-α), ERC and RIMS families of proteins, and that form trans-synaptic connections, such as the LAR receptor tyrosine phosphatase family members PTPRS and PTPRD47,48. Members of the Shank and Neurexin families of synaptic genes, as well as multiple genes involved in calcium signalling pathways (such as CADPS, CACNA1A, CACNA2D1, CACNB1 and RYR1), are also targets of the microexon-associated variants. Other predicted microexon-disrupting variants are in genes involved in signal transduction (such as SRC, PTK2, VAV2 and GIT2), vesicle trafficking (such as DCTN2, SYNJ1 and DNM1) and ubiquitination (such as NEDD4L, UBE2D3 and OS9). Overall, genes containing predicted impactful and ASD-associated variants, at the protein level, form an extended interaction network (Fig. 5), consistent with growing evidence that microexons often control protein–protein interactions that are critical for nervous system development and cognitive functioning3,4,49,50,51.

Neuronal microexons represent a critical hub of alternative splicing regulation, with characterized examples having defined roles in nervous system development and higher-order cognitive-functioning, as well as multiple links to brain disorders3,4. Here we reveal an expanded landscape of this important class of alternative splicing events that are directly affected by human variation. This variation is predicted to contribute to genetic burden underlying ASD, through disruption of microexon-dependent functions that converge on synaptic biology and additional neuronal pathways.

Previous studies using machine learning have provided evidence for an impact of genomic variants on splicing patterns in neurological disorders37,52,53,54. More-recent studies have further highlighted exons that are differentially spliced between individuals with ASD and unaffected individuals that are predicted to be affected by ASD-associated variants55,56,57. An important feature of our model is that it enables the inference of the collective impact of multiple variants on splicing and ASD association. Through analysing ASD-affected and unaffected individuals from the SSC, SPARK and MSSNG cohorts, our study highlights an extensively overlapping subset of microexons associated with variants that have a relatively large inferred disruption of splicing, with rare and ultrarare variants contributing the most to this signal. Moreover, predominantly the latter variants are associated with ASD status and enriched in a network of genes with previously established, as well as less well-defined or unknown links, to neurological disorders.

We observe that more than a third of individuals, independent of ASD status, have one or more variants predicted to affect microexon splicing, whereas a smaller proportion (2.5%) of ASD-affected individuals have variants predicted to disproportionately contribute to genetic burden associated with this disorder (Extended Data Fig. 10h). Analogous to other variant-associated mechanisms in ASD58, rarer variants are predicted to be more disruptive to microexon splicing than common variants, and are more often linked to ASD status. The majority (82%) of individuals carrying the ASD-associated variants (that is, from the RV-GDT superset) are idiopathic, whereas a smaller subset (18%) harbour previously detected8 candidate disruptive variants (Methods). Notably, similar to previously defined LGD variants59, a subset of the ultrarare variants predicted to disrupt microexon regulation concentrates in genes that function in ubiquitin-dependent proteasome degradation pathways (Fig. 5).

Our study offers a resource for a previously underappreciated source of rare genetic variation with potential to contribute to convergent mechanisms that underlie ASD, as well as common genetic variation that may, more broadly, affect individual-specific neurobiological differences. To facilitate further exploration of regulatory mechanisms and genotype–phenotype relationships involving microexons, we provide a repository of code predictions for all single-nucleotide variants and indels evaluated in this work (Supplementary Table 12). In the short term, this resource may inform the redesign of targeted genotyping approaches, such that they can capture microexon-disrupting variants (Extended Data Fig. 10j), and also facilitate the interpretation of future WGS data collected from individuals affected not only by autism, but also by other brain disorders in which dysregulation of microexons may have a role20. We expect this resource and insights from the present study to enable a deeper understanding of the multifaceted and critical functions of microexons in nervous system development, function and disorders.

To identify microexons that are differentially included during mouse development, we analysed embryonic brain RNA-seq data from ENCODE65,66 and whole-cortex data across various embryonic and postnatal developmental stages67. PSI measurements were grouped by neural tissue type (neural tube, hindbrain, midbrain, forebrain and whole cortex). For each microexon detected in at least five different developmental stages of a single tissue, we calculated the Spearman correlation of PSI across developmental time and associated P values. These P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction. A microexon was considered to be regulated through development if the adjusted P value was equal to or lower than 0.05 and the Spearman’s correlation was greater than 0, so that this definition selects microexons with increasing inclusion during development. Furthermore, we sought microexons with robust neuron-type-specific inclusion through downstream quantitative analyses of microexon PSI across pseudobulk pools derived from neuronal types extracted from the primary visual cortex and anterior lateral motor cortex24. Pseudobulk pools were grouped by their associated neuronal cell type. A microexon was defined as having an inclusion specific to a given cell type if at least two pseudobulk pools associated with that cell type exhibited a PSI with a lower bound binomial 95% confidence interval 20 units greater than the median PSI across all pseudobulk pools.

We analysed RNA-seq data from mouse embryonic and postnatal brain tissues (forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain, neural tube and whole cortex) spanning multiple developmental stages66,67 to identify microexons that are dynamically regulated during development. For each microexon and tissue, PSI measurements were modelled as a function of developmental time (days post coitum) using three approaches: (1) linear regression (ordinary least squares); (2) binomial generalized linear models (GLMs); and (3) piecewise binomial regression to capture non-linear relationships between PSI and time (implemented using the statsmodels module v0.14.2 in Python). Next, to train a classifier that detects neurally spliced microexons, we constructed a background set of intronic sequences that closely resemble annotated neuronal microexons in both length (3–27 nucleotides) and splice site strength. Splice site strength can be used to train an extremely highly accurate discriminator of microexons versus length-matched, randomly selected intronic sequences. Therefore, to incentivize the model to learn informative microexon splicing signals beyond splice site strength alone, such as RBP-binding sites and branch-point strength, we developed a sampling procedure to select a set of intronic sequences that match the bivariate 3′/5′ splice site strength distribution of known microexons.

To build this background set, we first downloaded genome annotations for all seven species in our data and extracted all intronic regions. To avoid bias from different levels of gene expression we only retained intronic sequences from genes that contain at least one neurally spliced microexon. We then identified a set of candidate microexon-like sequences in these introns, defined as all sequences between 3 and 27 nt that are flanked by 3′ and 5′ splice sites of which the strengths, as measured by MaxEntScan76, fall within the ranges observed for known microexons (−10 to +14 and −5 to +12, respectively), and that are not in the known microexon set. We then sampled from these candidate sequences to create the background set. We selected an initial set of 2 million candidate sequences for human, 1.6 million for mouse and slightly fewer for each of the other species.

To ensure that the background set matched the known microexons not only in the range of splice site strengths represented but also in their bivariate (that is, two dimensional) distributions of 3′ versus 5′ splice site strength, we used the known microexons to construct a two-dimensional target distribution for 3′/5′ splice site strength and then sampled the sequences for the background set from the candidate set according to this target distribution. To create the target distribution, we first separately estimated the one-dimensional marginal 3′ and 5′ splice site strength distributions of the known microexons using a kernel density estimator using the KernelDensity function from scikit-learn v.1.5.0 with bandwidth = 1. From these one-dimensional marginal distributions, we then created a discretized smoothed bivariate splice site strength distribution on a 20 by 20 grid that we used as the target distribution. These grid points were equally spaced within the splice site strength ranges indicated above (Extended Data Fig. 6a). We then binned the 3′ and 5′ splice site strengths of the candidate sequences on their respective grids to obtain normalized one-dimensional histograms of each marginal distribution. The outer product of these two histograms defined the normalized two-dimensional histogram used as the target distribution.

For each predicted feature we computed the maximum within each of the above intervals and concatenated them to obtain a total of 1,519 (=217 × 7) features.

COSSMO27 is a neural network trained to predict alternative 3′/5′ splice site selection. There are two versions of the model—one to predict alternative acceptor selection and one to predict alternative donor selection. We used the former model to predict the choice between the microexon’s acceptor and the downstream constitutive exon’s acceptor. We used the latter model to predict the choice between the upstream exon’s donor site or the microexon’s donor site.

After applying each model, we obtained the predicted probabilities to choose either the microexon or the flanking exon’s splice site, the logits (unnormalized splice site strengths) of these probabilities along with values of the model’s final hidden layer (200 values for each splice site). These final-layer values were used to capture a higher-order representation of the splice site learned by the model. This yielded a total of 808 ((2 × 200 + 2 + 2) × 2) features. COSSMO feature generation was performed using TensorFlow (v.2.2.0).

To obtain splice site strength features, we used the MaxEntScan algorithm76. Specifically, we used MaxEntScan to predict the strengths of the acceptor sites of both the microexon and the downstream flanking exon, as well as the donor sites of the microexon and the upstream flanking exon. Moreover, we used the difference in the strengths between the microexon and downstream acceptor scores and between the microexons and upstream donor scores.

We used svm-bp77 to predict the location and strength of both the microexon’s branch point and the downstream flanking exon’s branch point. We extracted 100 nucleotides of sequence upstream of both the microexon’s and the downstream flanking exon’s acceptor site and used svm-bp to identify the branch point candidate with the maximum strength in each of these sequences, and used their strengths, as well as their distance from their respective acceptor sites, as features (four total features).

We used the CisBP-RNA compendium of binding motifs for 117 human RBPs. We extracted the sequence of each microexon, as well as 300 nucleotides of flanking intronic sequence on both sides, and used the FIMO tool to predict scores for each motif at every position in the three intervals (5′ intron flank, microexon, 3′ intron flank). We next computed the maximum match within each of the three intervals and concatenated the values to obtain 351 total features.

In addition to PWM features from CisBP we also computed some motif-matching features for RBPs that have known roles in alternative microexon splicing, namely NOVA (motif: YCAY), CELF (motif: YGCUYK), STAR (motif: YUAAY), SRRM4 (motif: UGC) and SRSF11 (motifs: UCUCU or CUCUCU). To obtain our features, we separately counted the number of matches of each of these motifs in the microexon, as well as the ±300 nucleotides of flanking intronic sequence. Note that Y indicates C or U, and K indicates G or U. SRRM4 is a known microexon splicing factor with a well-defined binding region. Thus, we computed additional SRRM4 features as binary indicators of the presence of any of the motifs UGCUGC, CUGCUG, GUGCC, GUGCU or UGGACG in each 3 nucleotide bin between 60 nucleotides and 6 nucleotides upstream of the start of the microexon.

To obtain additional sequence features that are predictive of known microexons, we identified RNA hexamers enriched or depleted in the 100 nucleotide intronic flanking sequence upstream of known microexons versus the background sequences. We focused only on this 100 nucleotide interval because it is known to be the most critical for microexon splicing and extending the interval would have added too many features. We also limited this feature set to only the 100 most enriched and 100 most depleted hexamers. Specifically, for each known microexon or background sequence, we counted the number of occurrences of each hexamer in the 100 nucleotides upstream of the microexon in the training set. We next identified the 100 hexamers that are the most enriched in known microexons versus background sequences and vice versa. To compute features, we divided the 100 nucleotide upstream intronic sequence into five equal bins of 20 nucleotides and computed a binary value for each hexamer that indicates whether it is present in each bin, giving 1,000 (200 × 5) additional features.

We computed the fraction of G and C nucleotides within the microexon, within the 100 nucleotides of upstream intron immediately flanking the microexon, and within the 100 nucleotides of downstream intron immediately flanking the microexon. This gave a total of three features. The total number of features used by the model was 3,724.

All machine-learning models used in the input features had their trainable parameters frozen during training of the microexon code, so no information leakage between our train and test sets should be expected even in cases in which our test overlaps the training set of the component models. However, to rigorously assess potential information leakage from pretrained models, we evaluated overlap between our test sequences and training data of the pretrained models used in the features. COSSMO did not have any sequence overlap with our training set. While Seqweaver training regions overlapped 99.5% of our sequences, this does not constitute information leakage because Seqweaver was trained to predict general RBP profiles, not to classify microexons specifically. Our model learned to combine these general features into microexon-specific patterns. SVM-BP overlapped 11.0% (93) of foreground and 14.4% (2,583) of background. Excluding sequences overlapping SVM-BP training regions did not significantly affect classification performance (P = 0.42, Wilcoxon signed-rank test). Moreover, we used VastDB exon orthology annotations to identify test-set microexons whose orthologues were still present (after BLAT filtering) in the training set; removal of these microexons did not significantly affect performance (P = 0.11, Wilcoxon signed-rank test).

We trained the neural microexon classifier using gradient-boosted decision trees with a logistic objective function implemented in XGBoost v.1.5.0. The model outputs the probability that a candidate exon is a neurally expressed microexon. In addition to the held-out test set described above, which was used only to assess the model, we set aside 10% of the training data as a validation set to fit model hyperparameters and determine when to stop training. The model was trained for a maximum of 1,000 boosting rounds. Four metrics were computed on the validation set after every boosting round (classification error, area under the curve, AUPRC and log-loss); following the XGBoost convention that the last metric supplied in eval_metric governs early stopping, validation log-loss served as the early-stopping criterion. Training stopped when the validation log-loss failed to improve for 100 consecutive boosting rounds, and predictions were generated from the best-performing iteration. Remaining hyperparameters were a learning rate (eta) of 0.1, a maximum tree depth (max_depth) of 4, maximum Δstep (max_delta_step) of 7, minimum loss reduction to make a further partition on a leaf node (gamma) of 0.5, L2 regularization (lambda) of 0.1 and L1 regularization (alpha) of 0.8. To account for the imbalance between the positive and background sets, scale_pos_weight was set to the ratio of negative to positive training examples. After training, we evaluated performance on the held-out human test set using the AUPRC metric.

To interpret the contributions of individual features and feature groups to the microexon classification model, we performed SHAP (Shapley additive explanations) analysis78 using TreeExplainer with a background dataset of 2,000 randomly sampled instances from the background set. SHAP values were computed for all positively labelled foreground microexons against this background distribution. Feature-level importance was visualized using a SHAP summary plot displaying the top 30 features ranked by their impact on model output, with individual datapoints coloured by feature value to reveal the direction and magnitude of each feature’s effect. To provide a higher-level view, SHAP values were aggregated into interpretable feature groups (splice site strength, GC content, branch point features, RBP motifs, CisBP, COSSMO, Seqweaver, nSR100 and hexamers) by summing the per-instance SHAP contributions of constituent features within each group, and the resulting local group SHAP values were visualized as a grouped summary plot to illustrate the distribution and directionality of each feature group’s contribution across microexons.

To complement microexon code–model predictions, we evaluated variants within ±300 nucleotides of neuronal microexons present across analysed cohorts and represented on massively parallel splicing assay (MaPSy) data using the deep-learning splicing (CNN-based) predictors DeltaSplice (v.1.0.1)37 and Pangolin (v.1.0.1)36. DeltaSplice predictions were obtained using the brain-trained model and Pangolin predictions were obtained using tissue-specific models trained on brain, heart, liver and testis. For each variant, we extracted predicted changes in splice-site usage (ΔSSU) at both the 5′ and 3′ splice sites flanking the microexon. For downstream analyses, including correlation analyses with microexon MaPSy measurements, we considered the ΔSSU prediction with the largest absolute magnitude across the two splice sites as a single per-variant summary. Using these models, variants were classified as impactful if this maximum absolute ΔSSU was ≥0.05. Moreover, we used the Combined Annotation–Dependent Depletion (CADD; v.1.6)83 framework to obtain PHRED-scaled CADD scores, a genome-wide measure of predicted variant deleteriousness that integrates diverse annotations (including sequence conservation and regulatory features), and classified variants as having predicted pathogenic potential if their PHRED-scaled CADD score was ≥10.

We computed the differences in microexon code scores between 2,666 discordant sibling pairs from SSC and SPARK (ASD affected − unaffected sibling). To reduce the contributions of variants that were evenly distributed between ASD-affected and unaffected individuals, we implemented an imbalance filter: for each microexon, we grouped sibling-pair Δcode scores by their absolute value and required that at least two-thirds of sibling pairs sharing a given absolute Δcode score had the same sign (that is, ≥2/3 positive or ≥2/3 negative). We ranked the obtained microexon code score differences and extracted genomic variants associated with microexon code score differences within the top decile of the most positive and negative scores. For each variant, we calculated the maximum observed AF across general population cohorts sourced from gnomADv3 (ref. 44) and used this value to classify variants as ultrarare (max AF < 1 × 10−5) or rare (1 × 10−5 ≤ max AF < 1 × 10−2). Variants without frequency information were considered as common for downstream analyses. Furthermore, we assessed the evolutionary conservation of sites impacted by selected variants by calculating their mean phyloP score from the 100-vertebrate track within a ±5 nucleotide range and classified variants as overlapping highly conserved sequence if the mean phyloP score was ≥1.5. This comparison was restricted to intronic variants. To determine whether a subset of microexons is recurrently predicted to be impacted by rare or ultrarare variants across independent ASD cohorts, we applied a uniform variant-processing and scoring workflow to SSC, SPARK and MSSNG. Within each cohort, we computed microexon-level Δcode score values for each ASD-affected individual relative to a cohort-specific baseline, defined as the mean code score across unaffected siblings for the same microexon. We then decomposed each individual’s microexon Δcode score into the relative contributions of rare and ultrarare variants by aggregating single-variant |Δcode scores|. For each cohort, microexons were ranked by absolute Δcode score magnitude and we selected those in the upper decile (top 10%) of the most extreme differences, additionally requiring that the observed microexon Δ signal was predominantly driven by either rare or ultrarare variants (≥50% of the aggregated |Δcode scores| attributable to variants in the corresponding frequency class). The overlap across cohorts was quantified by direct set intersection of the selected microexon lists, including the three-way (SSC ∩ SPARK ∩ MSSNG) overlap. Statistical significance of the observed triple overlap was assessed by permutation testing (107 permutations), in which set sizes were preserved, and empirical P values were computed as (r + 1)/(n + 1), where r is the number of permutations yielding an overlap equal to or greater than that observed and n is the total number of permutations. The reported empirical P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Bonferroni method.

For each individual in discordant sibling pairs from SSC and SPARK, we computed a cumulative impact score as the sum of |Δcode score| values for variants classified as impactful, restricting to microexons that passed the ‘imbalance filter’ described above. Statistical significance of paired differences in cumulative impact scores between affected and unaffected siblings was assessed using a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. To assess robustness to the choice of impactful-variant threshold, we repeated the analysis across a range of |Δcode score| cut-offs expressed as multiples of the s.d. of the |Δcode score| distribution for common variants (Fig. 4f). We performed analogous cumulative-impact analyses using the CNN-based splicing predictors described above. For each model, variants were classified as impactful using the threshold corresponding to a ≥5% predicted splicing change: |Δcode score| = 0.29 for the microexon code-model, calibrated against MaPSy measurements (Extended Data Fig. 8d), and maximum |ΔSSU| ≥ 0.05 for DeltaSplice. These thresholds are indicated by vertical dashed lines in Fig. 4f. Moreover, we tested whether cumulative code score impact predicts ASD status using a sibling-pair-matched conditional logistic regression model, with ASD status as the outcome and sex as a covariate.

We performed generalized disequilibrium tests using RV-GDT (v.1.0)46 on sets of impactful variants derived from microexons that passed the ‘imbalance filter’ across analysed discordant sibling pairs. We evaluated three variant sets: (1) impactful variants prioritized by the microexon code-model; (2) impactful variants prioritized by DeltaSplice; and (3) the intersection of variants prioritized by both models. RV-GDT analyses were performed across a larger dataset comprising 25,132 individuals from 7,391 families drawn from SSC (n = 2,419), SPARK (n = 2,033) and MSSNG (n = 2,939). For code-model-derived variant sets, we incorporated variant weights to prioritize microexons of which the regulatory profiles most closely matched the neuronal microexon code, using the cluster 1 silhouette score derived from the ISM profile clustering described above. Empirical P values were obtained through within-pedigree phenotype permutations, as implemented in RV-GDT. For each tested variant set and allele-frequency stratum, we performed 10,000 phenotype permutations per run and repeated the procedure across 10 independent runs (total of 100,000 permutations per condition), summarizing results as the mean empirical P value across runs. Multiple-testing correction across RV-GDT tests was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, with significance assessed at FDR = 0.05.

Individuals carrying at least 1 variant from the 72-variant RV-GDT superset were identified across the SSC, SPARK and MSSNG cohorts (n = 629 carriers). Carriers were classified as idiopathic or as harbouring one or more previously detected candidate disruptive variants using participant-level annotations available for the SSC and MSSNG cohorts8; equivalent annotations were not available for SPARK participants. A participant was classified as idiopathic only if no previous finding was recorded in any of nine categories: SNV/indel findings with dominant inheritance; SNV/indel findings with recessive inheritance; chromosomal abnormalities; genomic disorders; large or gene-rich CNVs; structural variants disrupting ASD-associated genes; uniparental isodisomy; tandem repeat expansions; and mitochondrial variants. Of the 629 carriers, 162 had annotations available, of which 133 (82%) were classified as idiopathic and 29 (18%) had at least one previous genetic finding. Applying the same classification to all annotated individuals (n = 7,519) yields 85.7% idiopathic and 14.3% with previous genetic findings.
