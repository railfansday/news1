The edge of our solar system may be defined not by a sharp boundary, but by a "complex dance among forces, energies and trajectories," two researchers argue.

Two leading space scientists say there is a need to redefine the edge of the solar system by presenting a theory which states that measurements of the Milky Way's gravitational field, including its dark matter, redraws the sun's frontier.

That theory was put forward by Jim Green, former NASA chief scientist and past director of the space agency's Planetary Science Division, along with Ed Belbruno, a professor of mathematics at Yeshiva University and a senior research associate at Princeton University.

The solar system's gravitational frontier extends to nearly 3.8 light-years from the sun, they contend, roughly 241,000 astronomical units (AU), a distance that brings the "edge" of the solar system to within the same scale as the separation between neighboring stars. The edge of our solar system may be defined not by a sharp boundary, but by a "complex dance among forces, energies and trajectories," they write. Green and Belbruno recently made their case in the August 2026 issue of the magazine ROOM - the Space Journal of Asgardia.

Moreover, this theory claims that the gravitationally stable Solar Lagrange points L1 and L2 could form a type of "portal" that could funnel future interstellar visitors such as the recent comet 3I/ATLAS into the solar system, perhaps allowing scientists to capture them for future study.

"Discovering the solar-galactic center L1 location is important since slow moving objects that enter this region can be captured by our sun and become part of our solar system," Green told Space.com. "Who knows if there may be a large captured rogue planet on its way into the solar system right now," he said.

Green said there's a need to look at these newly calculated L1 and L2 locations to see what could be found. He is now in discussion about making such observations with the New Horizons spacecraft team that explored Pluto in 2015 and is now operating in the outer heliosphere.

"Even accumulated dust in this region could verify many of the calculations we have made including the extent of the dark matter in our galaxy," said Green.

"It's a really nice and significant result," Belbruno told Space.com. The ROOM article, written for a popular audience, draws from his August 2025 keynote lecture at the Niels Bohr Institute. Much of the theory also stems from Belbruno's work on permanent capture into the solar system and weak stability boundaries published in 2024 in the journal Celestial Mechanics and Dynamical Astronomy.

Belbruno authored the book Fly Me to the Moon: An Insider's Guide to the New Science of Space Travel (Princeton University Press, Princeton, 2007). In that work, he spells out how to harness low-fuel space travel he likens to "surfing the gravitational field."

"This new work represents a new application of weak stability boundary theory, first applied in 1987 to find new routes to Earth's moon, using ballistic capture for spacecraft," Belbruno said. "Now, its application is substantially extended to ballistic capture into our solar system from interstellar space and in estimating the size of our solar system," he said.

Green and Belbruno point out that the very recent discovery of interstellar objects  — such as interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS in 2025  — passing through our solar system on hyperbolic trajectories "has opened an entirely new window onto the material and processes shaping planetary systems across the galaxy."

The article in ROOM adds some impactful ideas regarding potential distant alien civilizations.

"Interstellar travel requires a mastery of gravity, which an advanced extraterrestrial civilization might use to tour the galaxy, using portals created at Lagrange points for leverage, Green and Belbruno write.

"Remaining at these locations, they could observe the life within a stellar system with essentially no energy expenditure," the researchers add, "so perhaps we should be looking for large space stations at stellar L1 and L2 locations as a techno-signature of advanced intelligent life?"
