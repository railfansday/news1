The Best Digital Wall Calendar (2026): Skylight, Everblog, Greenlight

I won’t lie to you: I hated the first digital wall calendar I tried.

I tested it last year and was left unimpressed. It was bulky and expensive, and my calendar details were slow to appear (if they appeared at all). The device felt more like clutter than a useful addition to my home. So, I wrote off the idea of using one at all, but to my surprise, digital wall calendars have only picked up in popularity and options since then. After trying a few new models myself this year, I can now see why.

These gadgets work by taking your online calendar—whether your Google Calendar, the calendar app on your iPhone, or your corporate Microsoft calendar account—and putting it on a digital screen that you can mount on the wall or prop up on a countertop or table. They’re often more than just calendars, also functioning as chore charts, meal planners, and sometimes even digital photo frames. I usually have smart displays all over my home, especially in my kitchen and living area, but for the past two weeks, I’ve replaced them all with digital wall calendars. And you know what? I don't miss talking to those annoying AI-powered assistants.

Instead, as someone who already lives and breathes by her Google Calendar, I love being able to see it on a large display in the middle of the kitchen, without needing my phone—especially when it’s on a digital wall device that immediately reflects my most up-to-date calendar. My husband and I have also been using the extra features more often, particularly to guide my 4-year-old through his evening bedtime routine with the help of his own companion device. Here’s everything you need to know about the best digital wall calendars and whether they’re right for you.

You might have heard these gadgets called by various names. Maybe “digital wall calendar” or “smart calendar device.” Maybe you’ve just heard the name Skylight Calendar, which is a notably popular model right now. No matter what you call them, these devices all do the same thing: replace a paper calendar with a digitized one that connects to the calendar tools you’re already using, like Google Calendar or Apple Calendar. The devices let you see your entire family’s schedule in one view, with the ability to update or add to it without needing a phone or a separate account. It also erases issues like unreadable handwriting and forgetting to write down an event you’ve already scheduled digitally, and its color-coding system makes it easy to see who’s on the hook for which activities.

While these are often called wall calendars, you can usually choose to place them on a tabletop. I preferred the models that included a tabletop stand, but I was still able to prop up the ones that only came with wall-mounting hardware during my testing.

I didn’t expect to feel this way, but having a physical version of my hyper-detailed Google Calendar in the center of my home has been a delight. My husband and I both check our calendar at a glance while we’re chatting in the kitchen, and at the start of the week, we’ll camp out in front of it for a few minutes to plan the week’s dinners and grocery needs. My top pick, Skylight Calendar 2, actually prompts us to add items to our grocery list based on what meals we picked (if it’s a stored recipe, that is).

My husband and I have found it so useful in part because we’re both good about adding events to our individual calendars, so now this device can reflect that work. It lets us skip pulling out our phones when discussing plans for the next day or the upcoming weekend.

Skylight’s calendar is the most popular for a reason. The brand is known for its digital photo frames, and now you can use the same Skylight app to operate any of its three digital calendars: The older 10-inch Calendar ($150), the newer Calendar 2 ($340), and the Calendar Max ($630). The main difference between these is size, but Calendar 2 is the newest model, with improved performance and interchangeable Snap Frames ($30 to $50) that you can purchase to change the device’s look (you can also buy these for the Calendar Max, but not the older 10-inch Calendar).

What makes Skylight great is its software. It’s easy to use, and it quickly imported my Google Calendar and the wide variety of calendars shared to it, so I could immediately add all of my family’s schedules to the device. The left-side menu bar shows all the pages you can toggle between. Calendar, Lists, Tasks, Rewards, Meals, and Recipes are the main areas you’ll use; you can also access the Photos, Sleep, and Settings pages from the same menu.

The app and device interface are clean and intuitive compared with other models, many of which feel like Android tablets with a fancy calendar mode added. I was able to easily assign calendars I created on the device to family members. For example, I assigned my husband’s calendar to him without needing him to log in or import it, and I could have assigned any calendar I created to my son as well. I also liked that shared events only appear once on the Skylight, but with both of our designated colors added to show the event in question applied to both of us.

Lists and Tasks sound similar, but Tasks are meant to function as a chore chart, connecting back to the Rewards page to build habits and earn the reward you set after hitting a certain goal. The main audience for this is children who are old enough to check the calendar. Lists are for things like your groceries. If you add recipes to the Recipe tab and then add those to your meal plan for that week, Skylight will offer to put the corresponding ingredients on your shopping list.

The Everblog HomeCal has an operating system that feels pleasantly similar to the Skylight. It has a bit of an Android undertone to it, but it doesn’t feel like a giant tablet as much as the Apolosign or Cozyla calendars (both below). Setup was pretty seamless, but there was a delay for the device to update with my calendar invites. It would also take a while for the Everblog to reflect changes I made to my Google Calendar on another device. While it worked well for the most part during testing, I occasionally noticed events missing from my Everblog. It also turned each calendar email account I connected to the device into a separate profile, which was annoying. I could toggle off the extra emails when I was on pages outside of the calendar, though, so I didn’t have three profiles for myself on my Chores page, for example.

The menu on the left-hand side is very similar to Skylight’s, with options like Calendar, Chores, and Meal Plan. There isn’t a recipe section, which I miss, but there is a Fridge section, which lets you track the groceries you already have. Part of why this exists is because Everblog also has a smaller, battery-powered version of this calendar that’s designed to attach to your fridge, aptly named the FridgeCal ($249). Still, I prefer the Recipe tab on the Skylight. The Everblog also has a Chores tab, but no additional tab for tracking regular chores or other to-dos separately.

There’s a free photo option, but the uploader tool in the app only lets you add one photo at a time. Painful, but I’m sure many would feel it’s worth the free price tag. To use the feature, you’ll need to go into the settings on the physical device to turn on photo slideshow as your screensaver. The Everblog defaults to pinging loudly at the start of any calendar event, which you can only change on the device, not in the app.

There’s a button on the bottom right-hand corner of the screen that will take you to a homepage with an overview of the calendar and chores, and access to other applications like Spotify and Netflix, if you set up your accounts on this device for streaming content. It comes enabled for wall mounting, but you can purchase the Calendar Stand ($39) if you want to situate it on a tabletop, and there’s a wooden frame ($99) you can buy to snap on to give it a nicer look.

The Greenlight Family Hub has a sleek, stylish interface that isn’t just made for family calendars and chores. It brings together various parts of the popular family finance app with the help of a calendar as a touchpoint, so it has a different feel than your typical digital wall calendar.

Greenlight started out as a tool to give your kids a debit card that you can easily track spending, and the company added chore tracking so parents could reward chores financially without needing to crack out their Venmo or have cash handy. Greenlight says parents then mentioned how they used the spending notifications to keep an eye on where their kids were, so the company added family location tracking through its apps, and launched a GPS tracker for smaller children who wouldn’t have a phone that could be tracked.

Now, Greenlight has launched the Family Hub to bring those features into a single device, plus a calendar. It’s made both for the family overall and for smaller children who don’t have a phone, so they can still use it to track their chores. The calendar aspect also works well and updates quickly to reflect changes I make on other devices, which I like.

It sounds a little Big Brother-y with all that tracking involved, but I understand the appeal as a parent who wants to be involved and keep an eye on things without expecting constant text updates. But I can feel the limitations sometimes with Greenlight’s approach to helping parents manage their kids, versus the entire family. Chores, for example, are only for children, and you can’t set up this hub without creating a child account with it. So if you’re just a married couple or household of roommates, you’ll be annoyed to list yourself as a child to use the chore tool.

But my favorite features aren’t what Greenlight is known for, at least not originally. This hub not only has free, unlimited storage for its photo screensaver, but also has the option to get voice support. That voice support is behind a paywall, requiring either $10 a month or $100 for the year, but it’s a feature I’ve long thought these devices need. It won’t connect to your smart-home devices, but will answer questions about the weather or your calendar and let you set timers, and it will prompt family members to add the parent PIN if they try to command any serious changes to the calendars or chore lists. Greenlight’s voice assistant worked well to understand my questions, but it was easy to trigger without actually saying the wake words “Hey Greenlight.”

You’ll need to make sure to follow the setup steps carefully if you don’t want to set up a financial account for your child. When I first signed up with the Greenlight app, I hit the button to create an account instead of choosing “Set up a Family Hub,” which got me stuck on the financial path. Greenlight’s support had to go in and reset my account for me, and it was spooky how fast the app pulled my legal name and information from just my phone number. Greenlight says it’s fixing this in its app so that it’s easier for people to back out and switch to hub setup only, but it’s something to look out for.

If you want a digital calendar made just for kids, Skylight has something along those lines called the Skylight Buddy. Originally it was just a chore and routine gadget, reflecting only his assigned tasks and habits from the Skylight app, but Skylight added a calendar feature so my son can now see the events tagged onto his personal calendar.

Even without the calendar feature, my 4-year-old loves his 7-inch Buddy, and it really does help get him to brush his teeth and put on his PJs in a timely (well, more timely) fashion now that it’s in his room. It also has a night-light and sound machine, but the sounds are limited, and the night-light is pretty dim. So for those capabilities, I’m sticking to the Hatch Baby my son is used to and loves.

Ultimately, this is most useful if you already have and use a Skylight Calendar, but now that it acts as a calendar itself, it can definitely be a stand-alone gadget. It highlights the routines front and center, but also makes it easy to see what events are coming up that day. My son’s schedule isn’t very busy at his age, nor can he even read his calendar invites at this point, but it’s a great add-on to an already useful device.

There are subscription options to get more features—Buddy Plus is $39 a year, and Skylight Plus is $79 a year. Skylight Plus is what you should get if you have the larger Calendar, as it unlocks paywalled features for the Buddy and Calendar devices. But I haven’t needed any subscription-only capabilities to keep my son excited about and engaged in his bedtime routine.

Apolosign’s digital calendar has three sizes: 15.7, 21.5, and 27 inches. It runs Google’s Android OS, so it operates very similarly to an Android tablet and to Google’s Pixel Tablet, if you’ve used either as a smart display, as I have. The Apolosign has two modes: Calendar and Android. Calendar mode worked similarly to Skylight. I seamlessly connected it to my Google Calendar, imported over most of my various shared calendars, and color-coded them.

The Calendar mode has a left-side menu bar (you’ll have to tap to make it appear), with sections for Home, Calendar, Routines, To-Dos, Rewards, and Meals. The Home page has a two-column design, showing you the calendar on one side and your Routines and To-Dos on the other. You can then tap into any of these sections or check off items directly from the Home page. Apolosign also has baked-in notifications that chime to remind you about calendar events and to-dos, plus phone notifications to let you know how many items are on your calendar that day.

WIRED Reviewer Kat Merck also tested Apolosign, but preferred Android mode to Calendar mode. Android mode allows you to customize widgets and layout, which was the only way she could see the monthly view she was used to from having a paper calendar. Android mode also lets you use photos from Google Photos as a background and screensaver. She says it’s nice to be able to customize the layout in this mode, but found that Android mode often struggles to sync with other programs, including Apolosign’s own app.

I don’t like the Apolosign experience quite as much as the Skylight or Everblog, and Kat agreed that this device is not very intuitive and takes a lot of patience to set up. But having no paywall is a huge plus, and many people will feel it’s worth getting used to the more Android-style experience to not pay a monthly or yearly fee. But since it’s essentially a large Android tablet, iPhone users have flagged on Reddit that they can’t two-way sync between it and their iPhone devices. The Apolosign is often on sale for the same price, if not cheaper, than the Skylight.

Cozyla Calendar Plus 2 (15.6 Inches) for $350: This calendar is similar to Apolosign with an Android-based operating system, but it didn’t import my shared calendars, meaning it took more work to set up. It was overall frustrating to navigate and didn’t guide me through its tools as effectively as Skylight or Apolosign. While Cozyla doesn’t have any subscription fees, its 15.6-inch size is more expensive than both Skylight’s and Apolosign’s.

Hearth Display for $629: This is another digital calendar and family tool that has a paywall with an affordable Family Membership ($6 a month) but a much higher initial price point. It’s only available in a large, 27-inch size, and I found it was slow to load my calendar and also bulky compared to more affordable options.

Digital wall calendars sync with your existing digital calendar app of choice, like Google Calendar, and display it on the physical screen. There are usually additional tools for recipes, to-do lists, and chores, but each digital wall calendar varies in how you import the information. You may have to manually import it or type the details into the device, but once you get used to the process, the function can be helpful for the whole family.

Will Changes to My Calendar Appear Instantly?

Changes you make on your native calendar app, like Google Calendar and Apple’s Calendar, should reflect quickly on the digital wall calendar device. But changes you make on the device aren’t always backward compatible with the native calendar. It depends on the calendar’s settings and how the information was shared; I can edit my primary personal calendar on the Skylight Calendar 2, for example, but I can’t edit my work calendar that I shared to the Skylight through my personal. There are also some syncing issues you may run into with the Apolosign, which doesn’t two-way sync with an iPhone since the digital calendar runs on an Android operating system.

How Is a Digital Calendar Different From a Smart Display?

Many smart displays look similar to these devices but have an entirely different operating system. Sure, the Google Pixel Tablet has some overlap with the Apolosign and Cozyla, since they all run an Android operating system. But unlike smart displays, digital calendar devices are designed with specific tools to run your family and none of the clutter. You’ll lose out on smart-home support, though Apolosign claims you can use Google Assistant on it (that hasn’t worked for me so far). On most devices, you’ll also compromise on extras like video streaming (you can, however, stream on Everblog). But as someone who uses a smart display regularly, I really didn’t miss it once I switched over to the digital calendar device. I do wish there were a weather-report page, as most digital wall calendars just show the current weather.
