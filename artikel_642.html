Nearly half of reef-building corals face elevated relative risk of extinction

Coral reefs are diverse marine ecosystems that provide ecological functions and economic and social benefits across over 100 tropical countries. Warming driven by climate change is a primary threat to coral reefs and is increasing the frequency of global coral bleaching events. Here we reassessed the IUCN Red List status of 892 reef-building coral species, first assessed in 2008 1. Because species-level population trends are unavailable for most reef-building corals, population reductions were inferred using proxies based on global coral cover trends and projected annual severe bleaching under SSP2-4.5, both over three decades, adjusted using trait-based estimates of species vulnerability. Of the 774 species with sufficient data to be assessed, 340 (44%) were classified as threatened under IUCN Red List criteria. Thirty-three species were assessed as Critically Endangered, 251 as Endangered, and 56 as Vulnerable, most due to risk of population reduction associated with projected thermal stress. These findings predate the three hottest years on record and the fourth global coral bleaching event (2023-2025), and the first exceedance for an annual mean of the 1.5oC global warming threshold. The findings reinforce the importance of limiting further warming to maintain coral biodiversity and reef ecosystem function. The Red List assesses the relative extinction risk of species, whereby species are placed in categories of increasing risk by comparing their population size, decline and geographic range against standardised thresholds. The Red List does not predict whether or when a species may go extinct. As the ranges of coral species remain under-surveyed, future assessments would be strengthened by species-level population data and thermal-stress and recovery metrics that better capture range-representative dynamics.

Supplementary text, Supplementary Tables 1–10 and references supporting the reassessment of extinction risk for 892 reef-building coral species. The text describes the methodological updates from the 2008 assessment: coral taxonomy, the interpretation of coral abundance and extinction risk under the IUCN Red List framework, coral cover as a proxy for population reduction, projected thermal stress, generation length, species traits and the Red List Index. It also reports supporting results on Red List criteria applied, genuine and non-genuine changes in classification, and geographic and taxonomic differences.

Species-level results and supporting data for all 892 species assessed. The workbook has two sheets. “Metadata” defines each variable, as in Supplementary Table 10. “Supplementary Dataset 1” gives, for each species: family, current and 2008 species names; Red List category and criteria for 2024, with recalculated categories for 2008 and pre-1998; realm, number of ecoregions, extent of occurrence and coral reef area within range; total and preferred depth ranges; projected population decline under SSP2-4.5 and past coral cover decline (GCRMN); scores for each vulnerability trait; mean, mode and final vulnerability scores.
